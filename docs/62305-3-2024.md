INTRODUCTION
This part of IEC 62305 deals with the protection, in and around a structure, against physical
damage and injury to human beings due to touch and step voltages.
The main and most effective measure for protection of structures and its content against
physical damage is considered to be a lightning protection system (LPS). It usually consists of
both external and internal lightning protection measures.
An external LPS is intended to
a) intercept a lightning flash to the structure (with an air-termination system),
b) conduct the lightning current safely towards earth (using a down-conductor system),
c) disperse the lightning current into the earth (using an earth-termination system).
NOTE The purpose of the external LPS is to protect the structure from a direct lightning strike by providing the
preferred attachment points, conducting and dispersing the lightning current. It will not significantly influence the
attachment process between the structure and the lightning to increase or reduce the number of direct lightning
strikes (S1) to the structure.
An internal LPS prevents dangerous sparking within the structure using either equipotential
bonding or a separation distance (and hence isolation) between the external LPS (as defined
in 3.2) and other electrically conducting elements internal to the structure.
Main protection measures against injury to human beings due to touch and step voltages are
intended to:
1) reduce dangerous current flowing through bodies by either insulating exposed conductive
parts, or by increasing the surface soil resistivity, or both,
2) reduce the occurrence of dangerous touch and step voltages by either physical restrictions
or warning notices, or both.
The type and location of an LPS should be carefully considered in the initial design of a new
structure, thereby enabling maximum advantage to be taken of the electrically conductive parts
of the structure. By so doing, design and construction of an integrated installation is made
easier, the overall aesthetic aspects can be improved, and the effectiveness of the LPS can be
increased at minimum cost and effort.
Access to the ground and the proper use of foundation steelwork for the purpose of forming an
effective earth termination may well be impossible once construction work on a site has
commenced. Therefore, soil resistivity and the nature of the earth should be considered at the
earliest possible stage of a project. This information is fundamental to the design of an earth-
termination system and can influence the foundation design work for the structure.
Regular consultation between LPS designers and installers, architects and builders is essential
in order to achieve the best result at minimum cost.
If lightning protection is to be added to an existing structure, every effort should be made to
ensure that it conforms to the principles of this document. The design of the type and location
of an LPS should take into account the features of the existing structure.
When safety is involved and significant changes are made to the structure or its use changes,
consideration of updating the lightning protection installation to the present edition of this
document is recommended.

1 Scope
This part of IEC 62305 provides the requirements for protection of a structure against physical
damage by means of a lightning protection system (LPS), and for protection against injury to
human beings due to touch and step voltages in the vicinity of an LPS (see IEC 62305-1).
This document is applicable to the:
a) design, installation, inspection and maintenance of an LPS for structures without limitation
of their height,
b) establishment of measures for protection against injury to human beings primarily due to
touch and step voltages.
NOTE 1 Specific requirements for an LPS in structures dangerous to their surroundings due to the risk of explosion
are provided in Annex C.
NOTE 2 This document is not intended to provide protection against failures of electrical and electronic systems
due to overvoltages. Specific requirements for such cases are provided in IEC 62305-4.
NOTE 3 Specific requirements for the protection against lightning of wind turbines are reported in IEC 61400-24
[1]1.
NOTE 4 Specific requirements for the protection against overvoltage of photovoltaic systems are reported in
IEC 61643-32 [2] and in IEC 62305-4:2024, Annex F.

3.3
isolated LPS
LPS with an air-termination system and down-conductor system positioned in such a way that
the LPS has no electrical and no physical contact with the structure to be protected except at
ground level for equipotential bonding and that along the complete path of the lightning current
the separation distance is respected
Note 1 to entry: In an isolated LPS, dangerous sparks between the LPS and the structure are avoided.
3.4
attached LPS
LPS with an air-termination system and down-conductor system and earth-termination system
positioned in such a way that the path of the lightning current can be in contact with the structure
to be protected
3.5
electrically insulated LPS
LPS with an air-termination system and down-conductor system attached to the structure but
positioned in such a way that the LPS has no electrical contact with the structure to be protected
except at ground level for equipotential bonding and that along the complete path of the
lightning current the separation distance is maintained
Note 1 to entry: In an electrically insulated LPS dangerous sparks between the LPS and the structure and partial
lightning currents are avoided.
Note 2 to entry: An insulating down conductor or insulating stand-offs that meet the requirements of IEC TS 62561-8
ensure that the LPS has no direct electrical contact with the structure to be protected and ensure an adequate
equivalent separation distance to avoid flashover.

A technically and economically optimized design of an LPS is possible, especially if the steps

construction of the structure to be protected. In particular, the design of the structure itself

The LPS should be designed and installed by well-trained and expert (or certified) LPS

The design of the class and location of the LPS for existing structures shall take into account
the constraints of the existing situation.
5 External lightning protection system
5.1 General
5.1.1 Application of an external LPS
The external LPS is intended to intercept direct lightning flashes to the structure, including
flashes to the side of the structure, and conduct the lightning current from the point of strike to
ground. The external LPS is also intended to disperse this current into the earth without causing
thermal or mechanical damage, or dangerous sparking which can trigger fire or explosions.
5.1.2 Application of an isolated LPS or an electrically insulated LPS
In most cases, the external LPS can be attached to the structure to be protected.
An isolated LPS or an electrically insulated LPS should be considered when the thermal and
explosive effects at the point of strike, or on the conductors carrying the lightning current, can
cause damage to the structure or to its contents (see Annex D). Typical examples include
structures with combustible coverings, structures with combustible walls and areas at risk of
explosion and fire.
NOTE 1 The use of an isolated LPS can be convenient where it is expected that future changes in the structure, its
contents or its use will require modifications to the LPS.

– 20 – IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024
An isolated LPS should also be considered when the susceptibility of the contents warrants the
reduction of the radiated electromagnetic field associated with the lightning current pulse in the
down conductor.
An isolated LPS or an electrically insulated LPS should also be considered if partial lightning
currents entering the structure can interfere with or destroy sensitive electrical or electronic
equipment.
NOTE 2 See more information in IEC 62305-4.
5.1.3 Use of natural components
Natural components made of conductive materials, which will always remain in or on the
structure and will not be modified (e.g. interconnected steel-reinforcement, metal framework of
the structure and metal sheets) can be used as parts of an LPS. When using natural
components as part of an LPS, the risk of damage to the natural components shall be
considered.
Other natural components can only be considered as being additional to an LPS.

5.2 Air-termination systems
5.2.1 General
The probability of lightning current penetrating a structure is decreased considerably by the
presence of a properly designed air-termination system.
Air-termination systems can be composed of any combination of the following elements:
a) rods (including free-standing masts);
b) catenary wires;
c) meshed conductors.
To conform to this document, all types of air-termination systems shall be positioned in
accordance with 5.2.2 and 5.2.3. All types of air terminals shall comply in full with this document.
For all types of air terminals only the real physical dimensions of the metal air-termination
systems shall be used for the determination of the volume protected.
The individual air-termination rods should be connected together at roof level to ensure current
division.
Radioactive air terminals are not allowed.

5.2.3 Air terminations against flashes to the side of tall structures
Lateral impacts of flashes to the side of tall structures are considered as negligible for structures

the facade can be endangered (e.g. balconies, cameras, antennas).
On structures taller than 60 m, flashes to the side can occur, especially to protruding parts, and
to corners and edges of facades.
NOTE 1 In general, the risk due to these flashes is low because only a few per cent of all flashes to tall structures
will be to the side and their parameters are significantly lower than those of flashes to the top of structures. However,
electrical and electronic equipment on walls outside structures can be destroyed even by lightning flashes with low
current peak values.
An air-termination system shall be installed to protect significant protrusions (such as balconies,
viewing platforms) on vertical surfaces on the upper part of tall structures (i.e. typically the
topmost 20 % of the height of the structure), as well as the equipment installed on them (see

of the vertical surface is optional.
NOTE 2 Elements protruding through the facade of structures up to 60 m in height are rarely endangered.

5.3 Down-conductor systems
5.3.1 General
To reduce the probability of damage due to lightning current flowing in the LPS, the down
conductors shall be arranged in such a way that from the point of strike to earth:
a) several parallel current paths exist;
b) the length of the current paths is kept to a minimum;
c) equipotential bonding to conducting parts of the structure is performed according to the
requirements of 6.2.
NOTE 1 Lateral connection of down conductors is considered good practice.
The geometry of the down conductors and of the ring conductors affects the separation distance
(see 6.3).

5.3.6 Test joints and test points
At the connection of the earth termination, a test joint should be fitted on each down conductor,
except in the case of embedded components used as down conductors combined with
foundation earth electrodes.
For down conductors embedded in concrete or using other natural components only a test point
is required.
For measuring purposes, the joint shall be capable of being opened with the aid of a tool. In
normal use, it shall remain closed.
Test joints shall be able to conduct lightning current and to withstand the electrodynamic forces
produced by the lightning current.
To fulfil the above requirements the test joints shall be tested according to 5.5.
5.4 Earth-termination system
5.4.1 General
When dealing with the dispersion of lightning current (high frequency behaviour) into the ground,
whilst minimizing any potentially dangerous overvoltages, the shape and dimensions of the
earth-termination system are important criteria. In general, a low earthing resistance (if possible
lower than 10 Ω when measured at low frequency) of the LPS earth-termination system is
recommended.
NOTE 1 Adding additional earthing electrodes or using earthing enhancing compounds according to IEC 62561-7
are methods available to reduce the earthing resistance, if necessary.
For protection against lightning, a single integrated earth-termination system is necessary and
is suitable for all purposes (i.e. lightning protection, power systems and telecommunication
systems, electronic and computer systems).
Earth-termination systems shall be bonded in accordance with the requirements of 6.2.
NOTE 2 Lightning equipotential bonding between the LPS earth-termination system and other earthing systems can
be achieved via natural components in accordance with 5.3.5 and 5.5.3.
NOTE 3 Serious corrosion problems can occur when earthing systems made of different materials are connected
to each other.

6 Internal lightning protection system
6.1 General
The internal LPS shall prevent the occurrence of dangerous sparking within the structure to be
protected due to lightning current flowing in the external LPS or in other conductive parts of the
structure.
Dangerous sparking can occur between the external LPS and other components such as:
– metal installations;
– internal systems;
– external conductive parts and lines connected to the structure.
NOTE For protection against overvoltages of internal systems, refer to IEC 62305-4.
Dangerous sparking between different parts can be avoided by means of
– equipotential bonding in accordance with 6.2, or
– separation distance between the parts in accordance with 6.3.
6.2 Lightning equipotential bonding
6.2.1 General
Equipotentialization is achieved by interconnecting the LPS with
– metal installations,
– internal systems,
– external conductive parts and lines connected to the structure.
When lightning equipotential bonding is established to internal systems, part of the lightning
current can flow into such systems and this effect shall be taken into account.
Interconnecting means can be
– bonding conductors, where the electrical continuity is not provided by natural bonding,
– surge protective devices (SPDs), where direct connections with bonding conductors are not
feasible,
– isolating spark gaps (ISGs), where direct connections with bonding conductors are not
allowed.
The manner in which lightning equipotential bonding is achieved is important and shall be
discussed with the operator of the telecommunication network, the electric power, gas pipes
operator, and other operators or authorities concerned, as there can be conflicting requirements.
SPDs shall be installed in such a way that they can be inspected.
When an LPS is installed, metalwork external to the structure to be protected can be affected.
This should be considered when designing such systems. Lightning equipotential bonding for
external metalwork can also be necessary.
Lightning equipotential bonding should be integrated and coordinated with other equipotential
bonding in the structure.

7 Maintenance and inspection of an LPS
7.1 General
The LPS should be maintained regularly, to ensure that it does not deteriorate and continues
to fulfil the requirements to which it was originally designed.
The effectiveness of any LPS depends on its installation, maintenance, and the testing methods
used. Inspections, testing and maintenance shall not be conducted during the threat of
thunderstorms.
NOTE Additional information on the inspection and maintenance of LPSs is provided in Clause D.7.
7.2 Maintenance
Regular inspections are among the fundamental conditions for reliable maintenance of an LPS.
The property owners or their designated representative shall be informed by the inspection
team of all observed deviations to the standard and they shall be repaired as soon as
reasonably practicable.
All maintenance work will be fully recorded and reported to the property owners or their
designated representative, to be kept with other information on the LPS.
Any repairs or upgrades to the lightning protection system resulting from change of the structure,
its use or its equipment should be carried out in a timely manner.
7.3 Objective of inspections
An LPS tends to lose its effectiveness over the years because of corrosion, weather-related
damage, theft, mechanical damage and damage from direct lightning strikes.
The mechanical and electrical characteristics of the LPS should be fully maintained throughout
the entire lifetime of the LPS in order to conform to the design requirements of this document.
The objective of the inspections is to ascertain that:
a) the LPS (internal and external) conforms to the design based on this document;
b) all components of the LPS are in good condition and capable of performing their designed
functions, and that there is no corrosion or other damage;
c) any recently added or modified services, equipment or constructions, or any modifications
on the building or its equipment or its purpose, are addressed into the LPS design.

– 48 – IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024
Inspections of lightning protection systems include visual inspections or electrical testing, or
both.
7.4 Need for inspection
Inspections should be made as follows:
– during the construction of the structure, to check the embedded electrodes or other
components which will be concealed in the structure and will become inaccessible;
– after the installation of the LPS;
– periodically at such intervals as determined with regard to the nature of the structure to be
protected, for example corrosion problems;
– after alterations or repairs;
– when it is known that the structure has been struck by lightning.
NOTE If no alternative exists, pictures of installations that are not accessible upon completion of the installation
can be accepted in lieu of inspection after installation. Examples include earthing connections and connections
in concealed locations.
During the periodic inspection, it is particularly important to check the following:
– deterioration and corrosion of air-termination elements, conductors and connections;
– earthing resistance (or impedance) value for the earth-termination system;
– condition of connections, equipotential bonding and fixing components.
All inspection and testing work and results shall be fully recorded and reported to the property
manager or his designated representative, to be kept with other information on the LPS.
8 Protection measures against injury to human beings due to touch and step
voltages
8.1 Protection measures against touch voltages
When, under normal conditions, people are present in the vicinity of the down conductors of an
LPS, there can be a hazard to life, even if the LPS has been designed and constructed
according to the requirements of this document.
The hazard is reduced to a tolerable level if one of the following conditions is fulfilled:
a) under normal conditions there are no persons within 3 m of the down conductors;
b) the "natural" down-conductor system consists of at least 10 columns of the extensive metal
framework of the structure or of at least 10 pillars of interconnected steel of the structure,
with the electrical continuity ensured;
NOTE 1 In such cases where the minimization of step and touch potential is essential, detailed calculations,
for instance by using computer programs, can be performed.
c) the contact resistance with the soil, within 3 m of the down conductor, is not less than 100 kΩ
over an area of a typical footprint (0,02 m2).
NOTE 2 A layer of insulating material, e.g. asphalt, of 5 cm thickness generally reduces the hazard to a tolerable
level.
NOTE 3 Contact resistance is as per definition IEV 581-23-08 [13]. Values are measured between a 400 cm2
electrode compressed with a uniform force of 750 N and a point where voltage is not modified by current injection.
If none of the conditions are fulfilled, protection measures shall be adopted against injury to
human beings due to touch voltages such as one of the following:

IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024 – 49 –
– insulation of the exposed down conductor is provided having a 100 kV, 1,2/50 μs impulse
withstand voltage, for example at least 3 mm cross-linked polyethylene; the withstand
voltage should be considered in wet conditions;
– either physical restrictions or warning notices, or both, to minimize the probability of down
conductors being touched.
Signage shall conform to the relevant standards (see ISO 3864-1).
8.2 Protection measures against step voltages
In certain conditions, the vicinity of the down conductors can be hazardous to life even if the
LPS has been designed and constructed according to the requirements of this document.
The hazard is reduced to a tolerable level if one of the following conditions is fulfilled:
a) under normal conditions there are no persons within 3 m from the down conductors;
b) the “natural” down-conductor system consists of at least 10 columns of the extensive metal
framework of the structure or of at least 10 pillars of interconnected steel of the structure,
with the electrical continuity ensured;
c) the contact resistance of the surface layer of the soil within 3 m of the down conductor is
not less than 100 kΩ.
NOTE 1 A layer of insulating material, e.g. asphalt, of 5 cm thickness generally reduces the hazard to a tolerable
level.
NOTE 2 Contact resistance is as per definition IEV 581-23-08. Values are measured between a 400 cm2 electrode
compressed with a uniform force of 750 N and a point where voltage is not modified by current injection.
NOTE 3 In cases where accurate assessment of step and touch potential is essential, detailed calculation using
computer programs can be performed.
If none of these conditions is fulfilled, protection measures shall be adopted against injury to
human beings due to step voltages as follows:
– equipotentialization by means of a meshed earth-termination system;
– either physical restrictions or warning notices, or both, to minimize the probability of access
to the dangerous area, within 3 m of the down conductor.
Signage shall conform to the relevant standards (see ISO 3864-1).
NOTE 4 The target of the measures against step voltages is to limit the maximum step voltage for human beings to
25 kV under an impulse current 10/350 µs for a 1 m step.

C.1 General
Annex C provides additional requirements for the design, construction, extension and
modification of lightning protection systems for structures with a risk of explosion.

C.2 Basic requirements
C.2.1 General
The lightning protection system shall be designed and installed in such a manner that, in case
of a direct lightning flash, there are no melting or spraying effects except at the striking point.
Sparks or damaging impact at the striking point can also be experienced. This should be taken
into consideration in the determination of air-termination device locations.
Down conductors that pass through a hazardous area shall be selected and installed such that
the temperature of surfaces exposed to the flammable atmosphere shall be below the ignition
temperature for the flammable gas, vapour or combustible dust and below the ignition
temperature of any materials in contact with the conductor.
NOTE The contact of a lightning stroke on electrical equipment cannot be avoided in every case.
C.2.2 Required information
The lightning protection system designer and installer shall be provided with drawings of the
structure(s) to be protected, with the areas in which explosive material will be handled or stored
or hazardous areas according to IEC 60079-10-1 and IEC 60079-10-2, appropriately marked.
NOTE It is a requirement of the IEC 60079 series that the details for the classification of hazardous areas are
documented. For explosive materials similar documentation would be prepared by the owners of the facility.
Information will be available to lightning protection systems designers and installers to enable proper design and
installation of lightning protection systems.
C.2.3 Earthing
For structures with a danger of explosion, a Type B earth-termination system according to
5.4.2.3 for all lightning protection systems, is preferred to improve equipotential bonding at the
base of the down conductors.
NOTE The construction of a structure can provide the effective equivalent of the ring conductor of a Type B

materials or with hazardous areas shall be as low as practicable and not greater than 10 Ω.

C.3 Structures containing solid explosive material
The design of lightning protection for structures containing solid explosive material shall take
into consideration the sensitivity of the material in the configuration in which it is used or stored.
For instance, it is possible that some insensitive bulk explosive material will not require any
additional consideration other than that contained within this Annex C. However, there are some
configurations of sensitive explosive materials that can be sensitive to either rapidly changing
electrical fields or radiated by lightning impulsive electromagnetic field, or both. It can be
necessary to establish additional bonding or shielding requirements for such applications.
For structures containing solid explosive materials, an isolated external LPS (as defined in 5.1.2)
is encouraged. Structures totally contained within a metallic shell of at least 4 mm thickness
steel or equivalent (7 mm for aluminium structures) can be considered as protected by a natural

D.4 Design of lightning protection systems (LPSs)
D.4.1 General remarks
The construction of an LPS for an existing structure should always be weighed against other
measures of lightning protection conforming to this document which give the same protection
level for reduced costs. For the selection of the most suitable protection measures, IEC 62305-2
applies.
The LPS should be designed and installed by LPS designers and installers. A thorough
knowledge of the relevant standards and several years of experience is required to become a
specialized designer or installer. The functions of an LPS designer and installer can be
performed by the same person.

mechanical effects of the lightning discharge and be familiar with the general principles of
electromagnetic compatibility (EMC). Furthermore, the lightning protection designer should be
capable of assessing corrosion effects and judging when it is necessary to seek expert
assistance.
The lightning protection designer and installer should be trained in the proper design and
installation of the LPS components in accordance with the requirements of this document.
Design and installation can be subject to national rules regulating construction work and the
building of structures.

D.4.2.3 Electrical and mechanical requirements
D.4.2.3.1 Electrical design
The LPS designer should select the appropriate LPS to obtain the most efficient construction.

D.5 External lightning protection system
D.5.1 General
D.5.1.1 Overview
The positioning of external LPS conductors is fundamental to the design of the LPS and
depends on the shape of the structure to be protected, the level of protection required, and the
geometric design method employed. The air-termination system design generally influences the
design of the down-conductor system, the earth-termination system and the internal LPS.
If adjoining buildings have an LPS, those LPSs, where permissible, should be connected to the
LPS of the building under consideration.
D.5.1.2 Dangerous sparking
Dangerous sparking between an LPS and metal, electrical and telecommunication installations
can be avoided
– in an isolated LPS, according to 6.3,
– in an attached LPS, according to 6.2 or 6.3.
All LPSs should always be connected to the equipotential bonding system at the ground level.
D.5.1.3 Types of LPS
D.5.1.3.1 Attached LPS
In most cases, the external LPS can be attached to the structure to be protected.
When the thermal effects at the point of strike or on conductors carrying the lightning current
can cause damage to the structure, or its contents, the spacing between LPS conductors and
combustible material should be at least 0,1 m.
NOTE Typical cases are structures with combustible coverings and combustible walls.
D.5.1.3.2 Isolated LPS
An isolated external LPS should be used when the flow of the lightning current into bonded
internal conductive parts above the ground level can cause damage to the structure or its
contents.
NOTE The use of an isolated LPS can be convenient where it is predicted that changes in the structure can require
modifications to the LPS.
An isolated LPS is achieved either by installing an air-termination rod atop a non-conductive
mast or metallic masts remote (not attached) from the structure to be protected, or by
suspending overhead wires between the masts in accordance with the separation distance of
6.3.
Conductive equipment within the structure and electrical conductors should not be installed with
distances to the air-termination system conductors and to the down conductors shorter than the
separation distance defined in 6.3. AII future installations should conform to the requirements
of an isolated LPS. These requirements should be made known to the owner of the structure
by the contractor responsible for the design and construction of the LPS.

The owner should inform future contractors performing work in or on the building about these
requirements. The contractor responsible for such work should inform the owner of the structure
if the contractor cannot meet these requirements.
The design of an LPS and the safety instructions for work in the vicinity of a roof fixture should
take account of the fact that the voltage on such fixtures will rise to that of the air-termination
system in the event of a lightning strike.
An isolated LPS should be installed on structures with extensive interlinked conductive parts
when it is desired to prevent lightning current from flowing through structure walls and internally
installed equipment.
On structures consisting of continuously interlinked conductive parts such as steel construction
or steel-reinforced concrete, the isolated LPS should maintain the separation distance to these
conductive parts of the structure.
It should be noted that columns and ceilings of reinforced concrete are often used in brick
structures.
Air-termination masts adjacent to structures or equipment to be protected are intended to
minimize the possibility of lightning strikes to structures within their zone of protection when an
isolated LPS is installed.
When more than one mast is installed, they can be interconnected by means of overhead
conductors and the proximity of the installations to the LPS should be in accordance with 6.3.
Overhead conductor connections between the masts extend the protected volume and also
distribute the lightning current between several down-conductor paths. The voltage drop along
the LPS and the electromagnetic interference in the space to be protected are therefore lower
than in the case when the overhead conductors are not present.
The strength of the electromagnetic field in the structure is reduced because of the greater
distance between the installations within the structure and the LPS. An isolated LPS can also
be applied to a structure of reinforced concrete, where the mesh provided by the reinforced
concrete will improve the electromagnetic shielding even more. However, for tall structures, the
construction of an isolated LPS is not practical.
Isolating air-termination systems made of stretched wires on isolating supports can be suitable
where a large number of extensive protruding fixtures on the roof surface are to be protected.
The isolation of the supports should be adequate for a voltage calculated from the separation
distance in accordance with 6.3. The design should take into account the sag in the stretched
wire that can be expected, including the consideration of high air temperatures.
To determine the influence of supporting structures on the separation distance for isolating
air-termination systems using high-voltage, insulating conductors see 5.5.4 and
IEC TS 62561-8:2018, Annex F.

D.5.2.2 Positioning
D.5.2.2.1 General methods
For the design of the air-termination system, the following methods should be used,
independently or in any combination, providing that the zones of protection afforded by different
parts of the air termination overlap and ensure that the structure is entirely protected according

a) rolling sphere method;
b) protection angle method;
c) mesh method.
AII three methods can be used for the design of an LPS. The choice of the method depends on
a practical evaluation of its suitability and the vulnerability of the structure to be protected.
The positioning method can be selected by the LPS designer. However, the following
considerations can be valid:
– the rolling sphere method is suitable for all structures;
– the protection angle method is suitable for structures not taller than the radius of the rolling
sphere relevant to the selected protection level of the LPS. Attention should be paid to
ensure the proper reference plane is used when assessing complex structures;
– the mesh method is for general purposes and it is particularly suitable for the protection of

D.5.2.3.1 Metal parts on structures higher than 60 m

D.5.2.3.2 Metal parts on structures below 60 m
All external metal parts that have a conductive connection with internal metal parts separated
by a distance d > s from a down conductor (or air terminations) should not be connected to the
down conductor so as to keep any unnecessary partial lightning currents out of the structure.
If this is not possible the metal part should be directly bonded to the LPS. The possibility of the
occurrence of partial lightning current into the structure should be considered.
Metal parts with a direct connection to the electrical or electronic installation inside the structure
should be protected with an air terminal on a distance d > s from that metal part. If it is not
possible to maintain that distance the metal part should be connected directly to the lightning
protection. Partial lightning currents in the connecting cables should be taken into consideration,
hence SPDs should be placed in the connecting cables at the zone border 0/1.

D.5.2.6.4 Protection of green roofs
Green roofs are roofs that are covered with a layer of soil and have plants on them for different
reasons. They come in two varieties where the main difference is in the accessibility of the
roofs. The park-like green roofs are designed to support people. The green roofs used for
thermal insulation are generally not meant to be walked on.
Even where protection is present as detailed in this subclause, it is advisable that at the
approach of a thunderstorm, people should leave the roof and seek shelter in a building.
a) Green roofs open to the public
These are generally characterized by walkways and larger plants and shrubs. Regardless of
whether or not a lightning protection system is installed for protection of the roof, signage should
be provided to warn people on the roof to seek shelter inside the structure at the approach of a
thunderstorm (e.g. by a thunderstrorm warning system (TWS)). The air-termination system for
these roofs should comply with the measures given below.
Structures with a roof layer of soil up to 0,5 m where people are regularly present, will require
a meshed air-termination system with meshes of 5 m × 5 m to prevent dangerous step voltages.
NOTE In such cases where the minimization of step and touch potential is essential, detailed calculation can be
performed.

IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024 – 87 –
To protect people who can be caught out on the roof from direct lightning flashes, air-termination
rods conforming to the rolling sphere method, can also be necessary. These rods can be
replaced by natural air-termination components, such as fences and lighting masts. The height
of the air-termination systems should take into account people’s height allowance of 2,5 m along
with the necessary separation distances. To protect people against dangerous touch voltages,
protection measures given in 8.1 should be implemented.
b) Green roofs not open to the public
These are generally meant for thermal insulation and covered by grass, sedum plants and the
like. The construction is composed of a watertight, electrically insulating layer with a thin layer
of soil and either a layer of mosses or small plants like succulents, or both. The optimal air-
termination system is a system of air-termination rods or meshes that are designed using the
rolling sphere method where the rolling sphere avoids the highest plants. The interconnecting
wires can be buried in the layer of soil. If the aesthetics are of major importance and hence a
visible air-termination system is not allowed, a buried mesh can also be applied. The width
should be no larger than 5 m × 5 m to prevent puncturing of the watertight layer in case of a
lightning strike in the centre of a mesh.
Materials used in the lightning protection system should not affect the vegetation or be affected
themselves. For this reason, the appropriate material to be used inside the roof covering is
stainless steel. Aluminium should not be used because of this reason and because of the
corrosion risk. Bare copper conductors should also be avoided for this reason and all copper
conductors inside the roof covering should be adequately coated in PVC or another moisture
barrier. Direct contact is necessary between the coating and copper conductor for example
when produced by extrusion. A PVC tube around a copper conductor creates more corrosion
than a bare copper conductor. Another possibility is to use tinned copper. Fixing components
should be non-metallic and all joints should be coated to prevent the ingress of moisture. Where
any copper conductor emerges through the roof covering for example finials, they should be
protected in the same manner to an adequate height.
c) Protection of structures covered by soil
For structures incorporating a layer of soil on the roof and where people are not regularly
present, a normal LPS can be utilized. The air-termination system should be a meshed
air-termination system on top of the soil, or a number of air-termination rods, connected by a
buried mesh, conforming to the rolling sphere or protection angle method.
Structures with a roof layer of soil up to 0,5 m where people are regularly present, will require
a meshed air-termination system with meshes of 5 m × 5 m to prevent dangerous step voltages.
To protect the people that can be caught on the roof from direct lightning flashes, air-termination
rods conforming to the rolling sphere method, can also be necessary. These rods can be
replaced by natural air-termination components, such as fences, lighting masts. The height of
the air-termination systems should take into account people’s height allowance of 2,5 m along
with the necessary separation distances (see also Figure D.17).
For underground structures with a layer of soil over 0,5 m, measures are under consideration.
As long as there is no research available, it is advisable to use the same measures as for layers
of soil up to 0,5 m.
D.5.3 Down-conductor systems
D.5.3.1 General
The choice of number and position of down conductors should take into account the fact that,
if the lightning current is shared in several down conductors, the risk of side flash and
electromagnetic disturbances inside the structure is reduced. It follows that, as far as possible,
the down conductors should be uniformly distributed along the perimeter of the structure and
with a symmetrical configuration. The current sharing is improved not only by increasing the
number of down conductors but also by equipotential interconnecting rings.

D.5.3.5 Natural components
D.5.3.5.1 General information
The use of natural down conductors to maximize the total number of parallel current conductors
is recommended as this minimizes the voltage drop in down-conductor systems and reduces
the electromagnetic interference within the structure. However, it should be ensured that such
down conductors are electrically continuous along the entire path between the air-termination
system and the earth-termination system.
Steel reinforcement of newly erected structures should be specified in accordance with 5.3.5 b).
If electrical continuity of the natural down conductors cannot be guaranteed, conventional down
conductors should be installed.

effective protection can be achieved against physical damage.

parallel paths. The impedance of the resulting mesh is thus low and, as a consequence, the
voltage drop due to the lightning current is also low. The magnetic field generated by the current
in the reinforcing steel mesh is weak due to the low current density and the parallel current
paths generating opposing electromagnetic fields. Interference with neighbouring internal

The following materials can be used as additional conductors installed in concrete for lightning

coated steel.

D.5.3.5.2.7 Connections
Investigations show that lashed joints (generally of a single wire twist, not bound as in
Figure D.23 c)) are not suitable for lightning-current carrying connections. There is a risk of the
lashing wire exploding and damaging the concrete. However, on the basis of earlier
investigations it can be assumed that at least every third wire lashing forms an electrically
conductive link, so that practically all the rods of the reinforcement are electrically
interconnected. Measurements carried out on reinforced concrete structures support this
conclusion.
So, for lightning-carrying connections, welding and clamping are the preferred methods. Lashed
joints as a connection are suitable for additional conductors for equipotentialization and for
EMC purposes only.
Connections of external circuits to the interconnected reinforcement should be performed by
means of clamps or by welding. Welds between reinforcing bars (see Figure D.23 a)) within
concrete should be at least 50 mm long. Crossing rods should be bent to run for at least 70 mm
in parallel prior to welding.
Where welding is permitted, both conventional welding and exothermic welding are acceptable.

D.5.4 Earth-termination systems
D.5.4.1 General
Earth-termination systems should perform the following tasks:
– conduction of the lightning current into the earth;
– equipotential bonding between the down conductors;
– potential control in the vicinity of conductive building walls.
The foundation earth electrodes and the Type B ring-type earth electrodes meet all these
requirements. Type A radial earth electrodes or deep-driven vertical earth electrodes are less
efficient with respect to equipotential bonding and potential control. If stringent constraints
about potential differences are to be observed, a foundation earth electrode and Type B earth
electrode may be preferred.
The LPS designer and the LPS installer should select suitable types of earth electrodes and
should locate them at safe distances from entrances and exits of a structure and from the
external conductive parts in the soil, such as cables, metal ducts. Hence the LPS designer and
the LPS installer should make special provisions for protection against dangerous step voltages
in the vicinity of the earth-termination networks if they are installed in areas accessible to the
public (see Clause 8).
The recommended value of the overall earth resistance of 10 Ω is fairly conservative in the case
of structures in which direct equipotential bonding is applied. The resistance value should be
as low as possible in every case but especially in the case of structures associated with
hazardous areas or explosive materials. Still the most important measure is equipotential
bonding.
The embedded depth and the type of the earth electrodes should be such as to minimize the
effects of corrosion, soil drying and freezing and thereby stabilize the earth resistance. It is
recommended that the first half metre of a vertical earth electrode should not be regarded as
being effective under frost conditions.
Deep-driven earth electrodes can be effective in special cases where soil resistivity decreases
with depth and where substrata of low resistivity occur at depths greater than those to which
rod electrodes are normally driven.
Metals used for earth electrodes should conform to the materials listed in Table 8, and the
behaviour of the metal with respect to corrosion in the soil should always be taken into account.
Some guidance is given in 5.6. When guidance for particular soils is not available, the
experience with earth-termination systems in neighbouring plants, with soil exhibiting similar
chemical properties and consistency, should be ascertained. When the trenches for earth
electrodes are refilled, care should be taken that no fly ash, lumps of coal or building rubble is
in direct contact with the earth electrode.
If the metal reinforcement is also used for the protective earth, the most severe measure in
respect of thickness of the rods and the connection should be chosen. In this case, larger sizes
of reinforcement bars or superimposed mesh conductors can be considered. The need for short
and straight connections for the lightning protection earthing should be recognized at all times.

D.5.4.2 Types of earth electrode arrangements
D.5.4.2.1 General
Subclause 5.4.2 provides details on the design and installation of two basic types of earth
electrode arrangements that can be used; a Type A arrangement that consists of either vertical
or horizontal earth electrodes, or both, and a Type B arrangement that consists of either a ring
conductor external to the structure or a foundation earth electrode. The Type B arrangement
forms a closed loop and in a Type A arrangement it is uncommon to do so.
D.5.4.2.2 Type A arrangement
The Type A earth-termination system is suitable for low structures (e.g. family houses), existing
structures or an isolated LPS with rods or stretched wires. This type of arrangement comprises
horizontal or vertical earth electrodes connected to each down conductor.
Where there is a ring conductor (which interconnects the down conductors) in contact with the
soil, the earth electrode arrangement is classified as Type A if the ring conductor is in contact
with the soil for less than 80 % of its length.
In a Type A arrangement, the minimum number of earth electrodes should be one for each down
conductor and a minimum of two for the LPS.
D.5.4.2.3 Type B arrangement
D.5.4.2.3.1 Application
The Type B earth-termination system is preferred for meshed air-termination systems and for
LPSs with several down conductors. This type of arrangement comprises either a ring earth
electrode external to the structure, in contact with the soil for at least 80 % of its total length,
or a foundation earth electrode.
For bare solid rock, only the Type B earthing arrangement is recommended.

D.5.4.3.1 General
The structure foundations of interconnected steel-reinforced concrete should be used as
foundation earth electrodes. They exhibit very low earthing resistance and perform an excellent
equipotentialization reference. When this is not possible, an earth-termination system,

D.5.4.3.2 Foundation as earth termination
For large structures and industrial plants, the foundation is normally reinforced. The reinforcing
rods of the foundation, foundation slab and outer walls in the region below the soil surface of
such structures form an excellent foundation earth electrode, provided the requirements of 5.4
are satisfied.
The reinforcing rods of the foundation and the buried walls can be used as a foundation earth
electrode.
This method achieves good earthing at minimum cost. In addition, the metal enclosure,
consisting of the steel reinforcement of the structure, in general offers a good potential
reference for the electric power supply, telecommunication and electronic installations of the
structure.
In addition to the interconnection of the reinforcing rods by wire-lashing, the installation of an
additional meshed metal network to ensure good connections is recommended. This additional
network should also be lashed to the reinforcement steel. The terminal conductors for
connections of external down conductors or structure elements used as down conductors and
for connection of the earth termination installed externally should be brought out of the concrete
at suitable points.
In general, the reinforcing of a foundation is electrically conductive except in cases where gaps
are provided between different parts of the structure to allow different settling rates.
Gaps between conductive structure parts should be bridged by bonding conductors conforming
to Table 7 using clamps and joints in accordance with 5.5.
Reinforcing rods of concrete columns and walls standing on a foundation should be connected
to the reinforcing rods of the foundation and to the conductive parts of the roof.
When welding to reinforcing is not allowed, additional conductors should be installed in the
columns, or the connections should be implemented by means of tested joints. These additional
conductors should be bound or clamped to the reinforcing steel.
After completion of construction and connecting all the services to the building via an
equipotential bonding bar, it will often be impossible (in practice) to measure the earthing
resistance as part of the maintenance programme.
If, in certain conditions, it is not possible to measure the earthing resistance of the foundation
earth, the installation of one or more reference earth electrodes close to the structure provide
a possible method of monitoring the changes in the environment of the earthing system over
the years by performing a circuit measurement between the earth electrode and the foundation
earthing system. However, good equipotentialization is the main advantage of the foundation
earthing system and the resistance to earth tends to be less important.

D.5.4.3.5 Type B – Ring earth electrodes
For structures using isolating material such as brickwork or wood with no steel-reinforced
foundation, a Type B earth termination conforming to 5.4.2.3 is preferable. In order to reduce
the earth resistance, the Type B earthing arrangement can be improved, if necessary, by adding
vertical earth electrodes, or radial earth electrodes conforming to 5.4.2.3. Figure 5 gives the
requirements regarding the minimum length of earth electrodes.
The clearance and depth for a Type B earth electrode, as stated in 5.4.3, is suitable in normal
soil conditions for the protection of persons in the vicinity of the structure. In countries with low
winter temperatures, the appropriate depth of earth electrodes should be considered.
Type B earth electrodes also perform the function of potential equalization between the down
conductors at ground level, since the various down conductors give different potentials as a
result of the unequal distribution of lightning currents due to variations in the earth resistance
and different lengths in the above ground conductor current paths. The different potentials result
in a flow of equalizing currents through the ring earth electrode, so that the maximum rise in
potential is reduced and the equipotential bonding systems connected to it within the structure,
are brought to approximately the same potential.
Where structures belonging to different owners are built closely to each other, it is often not
possible to install a ring earth electrode that will fully surround each structure. In this case the
efficiency of the earth-termination system is somewhat reduced, since the conductor ring acts
partly as a Type B electrode, partly as a foundation earth and partly as an equipotential bonding
conductor.
Where large numbers of people frequently assemble in an area adjacent to a structure to be
protected, further potential control for such areas should be provided. More ring earth
electrodes should be installed at distances of approximately 3 m from the first and any
subsequent ring conductors. Ring electrodes further from the structure should be installed more
deeply below the surface i.e. those at 4 m from the structure at a depth of 1 m, those at 7 m
from the structure at a depth of 1,5 m and those at 10 m from the structure at a depth of 2 m.
These ring earth electrodes should be connected to the first ring conductor by means of radial
conductors.
When the area adjacent to the structure is covered with a 50 mm thick layer of asphalt of low
conductivity, sufficient protection is provided for people making use of the area.
D.5.4.3.6 Earth electrodes in rocky soil
During construction, a foundation earth electrode should be built into the concrete foundation.
Even where a foundation earth electrode has a reduced earthing effect in rocky soil, it still acts
as an equipotential bonding conductor.
At the test joints, additional earth electrodes should be connected to the down conductors and
foundation earth electrodes.
Where a foundation earth electrode is not provided, a Type B arrangement (a ring earth
electrode) should be used instead. If the earth electrode cannot be installed in the soil and has
to be mounted on the surface, it should be protected against mechanical damage.
Radial earth electrodes lying on or near the earth surface should be covered by stones, or
embedded in concrete for mechanical protection.

D.5.4.3.7 Earth-termination systems in large areas
An industrial plant typically comprises a number of associated structures, between which many
power and signal cables are installed.
The earth-termination systems of such structures are very important for the protection of the
electrical system. A low impedance earth system reduces the potential difference between the
structures and so reduces the interference injected into the electrical links.
A low earth impedance can be achieved by providing the structures with foundation earth
electrodes and additional Type B and Type A earth arrangements conforming to 5.4.
Interconnections between the earth electrodes, the foundation earth electrodes and the down
conductors should be installed at the test joints. Some of the test joints should also be
connected to the equipotential bars of the internal LPS.
Internal down conductors, or internal structural parts used as down conductors, should be
connected to an earth electrode and the reinforcement steel of the floor to avoid step and touch
voltages. If internal down conductors are near expansion joints in the concrete, these joints
should be bridged as near to the internal down conductor as possible.
The lower part of an exposed down conductor should be insulated by PVC tubing with a
thickness of at least 3 mm or with equivalent insulation.
In order to reduce the probability of direct lightning flashes to cable routes in the ground, an
earthing conductor and, in the case of wider cable routes, a number of earthing conductors
should be installed above the cable routes.
By interconnecting the earths of a number of structures, a meshed earthing system is obtained.

D.5.6.2.2 Protection against corrosion
D.5.6.2.2.1 General
The LPS should be constructed of corrosion-resistant materials such as copper, aluminium,
stainless steel and galvanized steel. The material of the air-termination rods and air-termination
wires should be electrochemically compatible with the material of the connection and mounting
elements, and it should have a good corrosion resistance to a corrosive atmosphere or to
moisture.
Connections between different materials should be avoided; otherwise, they should be
protected. Copper parts should never be installed above galvanized or aluminium parts unless
those parts are provided with protection against corrosion.
Extremely fine particles are shed by copper parts which result in severe corrosive damage to
galvanized parts even where the copper and galvanized parts are not in direct contact.
Aluminium conductors should not be directly attached to calcareous building surfaces such as
concrete limestone and plaster, and should never be used in soil.
D.5.6.2.2.2 Metals in soil and air
Corrosion of metal will occur at a rate depending on the type of metal and the nature of its
environment. Environmental factors such as moisture, dissolved salts (thus forming an
electrolyte), degree of aeration, temperature and extent of movement of electrolyte combine to
make this condition a very complex one.
In addition, local conditions, with different natural or industrial contaminants, can cause
significant variations as observed in different parts of the world. To resolve particular corrosion
problems, consultation with corrosion specialists is strongly recommended.
The effect of contact between dissimilar metals, in association with a surrounding, or partially
surrounding, electrolyte, will lead to increased corrosion of the more anodic metal, and to
decreased corrosion of the more cathodic metal. Material of the earthing system, which is
directly connected to steel reinforcement in concrete, should be selected in consideration of the
galvanic potential difference between the metal materials.
The corrosion of the more cathodic metal will not necessarily be fully prevented. The electrolyte
for this reaction can be groundwater, soil with some moisture content or even moisture
condensate in above-ground structures where it is retained by crevices.

– 120 – IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024
Extended earthing systems can suffer from different ground conditions in different parts. These
can enhance the corrosion problems and can require special attention. In order to minimize
corrosion in an LPS:
– avoid the use of unsuitable metals in an aggressive environment;
– avoid contact of dissimilar metals, of substantially differing electrochemical or galvanic
activity;
– use an adequate cross-section of conductors, bonding straps and conducting terminals and
clamps to ensure sufficient corrosion life for the conditions of service;
– provide appropriate filling or isolating material in conductor joints which have not been
welded, so as to exclude moisture;
– provide a sleeve or a coat or otherwise protect metals sensitive to corrosive fumes or fluids
in the location of the installation;
– consider the galvanic effects of other metallic items to which the earth electrode is to be
connected;
– avoid designs where natural corrosion products from a cathodic metal (e.g. copper) can
contact and erode the LPS, such as metallic copper on an anodic metal (e.g. steel or
aluminium).
To conform to the foregoing, the following precautions are cited as specific examples:
– the minimum thickness or diameter of a strand should be 1,7 mm for steel, aluminium,
copper, cuprous alloy or nickel/chrome/steel alloys;
– an isolating spacer is recommended where contact between closely spaced (or touching)
dissimilar metals can cause corrosion, but such contact is not electrically necessary;
– steel conductors not otherwise protected should be hot-dipped galvanized in accordance
with the requirements of Table 7 and Table 8;
– aluminium conductors should not be buried directly in the ground, set in gravel nor set in or
directly attached to concrete, limestone or lime mortar unless they are completely sleeved
with a durable, close-fitting isolating sleeve;
– copper to aluminium joints should be avoided wherever possible. In cases where they cannot
be avoided, the connections should be welded or be made employing an intermediate layer
of copper to aluminium sheet;
– direct copper to aluminium joints should be avoided wherever possible or interconnections
should be made through the use of purpose-made bi-metallic joints conforming to
IEC 62561-1;
– fasteners or sleeves for aluminium conductors should be of similar metal or of UV-resistant
PVC and of adequate cross section to avoid failure by adverse weather conditions;
– copper is suitable for use in most earth electrode applications, except in acidic, oxygenated
ammoniac or sulphurous conditions. However, it will cause galvanic damage to ferrous
materials to which it is bonded. This can require specialist corrosion advice, particularly
when a cathodic protection scheme is used;
– for roof conductors and down conductors exposed to aggressive flue gases, protection
against corrosion should be considered, for example through the use of high-alloy steels
(chromium ≥ 16 %; nickel ≥ 8 %; carbon ≤ 0,08 %);
– stainless steel or other nickel alloys can be used for the same corrosion resistance
requirements. However, in anaerobic conditions, such as clay, they will corrode almost as
quickly as mild steel;
– joints between steel and copper or copper alloys in air, if not welded, should be either fully
tin-plated or fully coated with a durable moisture-resistant coating;
– copper and copper alloys are subject to stress corrosion cracking in ammoniac fumes and
these materials should not be used for fastenings in these specific applications;
– in marine and coastal areas, all conductor joints should be welded or effectively fully sealed.

Stainless steel or copper earthing systems can be connected directly to the steel reinforcement
in concrete.
Galvanized steel earth electrodes in soil should be connected to the steel reinforcement in
concrete by isolating spark gaps capable of conducting a substantial part of the lightning current
(see Table 9 and Table 10 for the dimensions of connecting conductors). A direct connection in
the soil would significantly increase the risk of corrosion. For information on isolating spark
gaps, see 6.2.
Galvanized steel should only be used for earth electrodes in soil when no steel parts
incorporated in the concrete, are directly connected to the earth electrode in soil.
If metal pipes are put in soil and are connected to the equipotential bonding system and to the
earth-termination system, the material of the pipes, where these are not isolated, and the
material of the conductors of the earth system should be identical. Pipes with a protective
covering of paint or asphalt should be treated as if they are not isolated. When use of the same
material is not possible, the pipework system should be isolated from the plant sections
connected to the equipotential bonding system by means of isolating sections. The isolating
sections should be bridged by means of spark gaps. Bridging by spark gaps should also be
performed where isolating pieces are installed for cathodic protection of pipework.
Conductors with lead sheaths should not be installed directly in concrete. Conductors with lead
sheaths should be protected against corrosion by provision of either anti-corrosion bindings or
by means of shrunk-on sleeving. Conductors may be protected by a PVC or PE covering.
Steel earth-termination conductors coming from concrete or from soil at the entry point to the
air should be protected against corrosion for a length of 0,3 m by means of anti-corrosion
wrappings or shrunk-on sleeving. For copper, copper-clad steel or stainless steel conductors
this is not necessary.
The materials used for the joints between conductors in the soil should have identical corrosion
behaviour as the conductors or they shall be protected against corrosion. Connection by
clamping is permissible in cases where such connections are provided with effective corrosion
protection after making the joint. Good experience has been gained with crimped joints. Welded
joints avoid corrosion if the employed material is the same as the conductors to be connected.
Practical experience shows that:
• aluminium should never be used as an earth electrode,
• lead-sheathed steel conductors are not suitable for use as earth conductors,
• lead-sheathed copper conductors should not be used in concrete nor in soil with a high
calcium content.
D.5.6.2.2.3 Metals in concrete
The embedding of steel in concrete causes a stabilization of the natural potential of the metal,
due to the uniformly alkaline environment. In addition, the concrete is of uniformly, relatively
high resistivity – of the order of 200 Ω · m or higher.
Consequently, the reinforcing bars in concrete are considerably more resistant to corrosion than
when they are exposed, even if connected externally to more cathodic-electrode materials.
The use of reinforcing steel as down conductors does not pose any significant corrosion
problems provided the access points for air terminations are well encapsulated, for example by
epoxy resin putty of adequate thickness.

Galvanized steel strips as foundation earth electrodes can be installed in concrete and directly
connected to the steel reinforcing rods. Copper, copper-coated steel and stainless steel in
concrete are also accepted and can be connected to the reinforcement steel directly.
Owing to the natural potential of steel in concrete, additional earth electrodes outside the
concrete should be made of copper, copper-coated steel or stainless steel, considering the
galvanic potential differences between the metal materials.
In steel fibre reinforced concrete, if it is not possible to ensure the circumfusion of concrete
thickness at least 50 mm over casting earth electrodes, the use of steel earth electrodes is not
permitted because during the building process the steel electrode can be pressed down, for
instance by the machines used, and touch the soil. In such a case, the steel faces a serious
corrosion risk. Copper and stainless steel are suitable materials for earth electrodes in steel
fibre concrete.
D.6 Internal lightning protection system
D.6.1 General
The requirements for the design of the internal lightning protection system are given in Clause 6.
The external lightning protection system and its relationship to conductive parts and
installations inside the structure will determine, to a large extent, the need for an internal
lightning protection system.
Consultation with all parties concerned with equipotential bonding is essential. The LPS
designer and LPS installer should make it clear to the general contractor or LPS purchaser that
the measures given in Clause 6 and discussed in Clause D.6 are very important in order to
achieve adequate lightning protection for internal systems.
The measures necessary for internal lightning protection exceed the equipotentialization
measures for AC power systems in many cases due to high peak currents and high current rise
times resulting from direct and nearby lightning strikes. The installation of SPDs tested to class
I for power lines shall be done in accordance with IEC 60364-5-53. See also the requirements
of IEC 62305-4 for protection of electrical and electronic systems.
D.6.2 Lightning equipotential bonding (EB)
D.6.2.1 General
D.6.2.1.1 Overview
In the cases of an isolated external LPS and of an electrically insulated LPS the equipotential
bonding is established only at ground level.
NOTE In structures with metallic framework or reinforced concrete framework, which is electrically-continuous
connected, these parts of the structure (including the roof) can be considered as an equipotential surface. A

ground level.
In the case of industrial structures, electrically-continuous conductive parts of the structure and
the roof can be generally used as natural LPS components and can be used in the performance

D.6.2.1.3 Surge protective devices
Surge protective devices (SPDs) should withstand the prospective part of the lightning current
flowing through them without being damaged. An SPD should also have the ability to extinguish
electrical power follow-on currents from the power supply if they are connected to the electrical
power conductors.
The requirements for the selection of an SPD used for equipotentialization with internal systems
is given in 6.2. For information regarding LEMP, see IEC 62305-4.
D.6.2.2 Equipotential bonding of internal conductive parts
Bonding should be provided and installed in such a way that the internal conductive parts, the
external conductive parts and the electrical power and communication systems (e.g. computers
and security systems) can be bonded by short bonding conductors. Internal and external
conductive parts that have no electrical function should be bonded directly. All electrical
connections (power and signal) should be bonded by means of SPDs.
Metal installations, i.e. water, gas, heating and air pipes, lift shafts, crane supports, etc. are
required to be bonded together and to the LPS at ground level.
Sparking can occur in metal parts not belonging to the structure if those parts are close to the
down conductors of the LPS. Where this is considered dangerous, adequate bonding measures
according to 6.2 should be used to prevent sparking.

D.6.4 Protection against effects of induced currents in internal systems
Currents in the conductors of the external LPS can induce excessive overvoltages in the
conductor loops of the internal installations by the effect of magnetic coupling. Overvoltages
can cause failures of internal systems.
Since practically all buildings contain electronic equipment, the effect of the electromagnetic
field of the external and internal down conductors should be taken into account in the planning
of the lightning protection system.
Protection measures against overvoltages are given in IEC 62305-4.
D.7 Maintenance and inspection of the LPS
D.7.1 General
Main information on inspection, testing and maintenance is given in Clause 7.
The need for upgrading to conform to new requirements in published standards should be
carried out as necessary, taking into account that this practice can be subject to additional
national regulations.
Additional requests on the type and frequency of inspection, testing and maintenance for an
LPS can come from the owner (under insurance, LPS builder or designer, or other interested
parties’ indication) or can be identified in other specifications such as construction guidelines,
technical regulations, technical instructions, and industrial internal safety procedures.
Additional requests are only acceptable if they are more stringent than the requirements laid
out in national regulations.
Maintenance of an LPS is important even though the LPS designer has taken special
precautions to provide corrosion protection and has dimensioned the LPS components
according to their particular exposure to lightning damage and weather elements in addition to
the other requirements of this document.
D.7.2 Inspection
D.7.2.1 General
The purpose of inspections is to ensure that the LPS conforms to this document in all respects.
The inspection includes checking technical documentation before visual inspections, testing
and logging in an inspection report. Inspections of lightning protection systems include either
visual inspections or electrical testing, or both.
NOTE This does not require that all inspections consist of both a visual inspection and electrical testing.
Inspection of the LPS should be conducted by a lightning protection specialist in accordance
with the recommendations of Clause 7 and Clause D.7. The inspector should be provided with
previous LPS maintenance and inspection reports along with the LPS design report containing
the necessary documentation of the LPS such as design criteria, design description and
technical drawings.
For inspections associated with structures with a risk of explosion, as described in Annex C, it
would be necessary for the LPS inspection personnel to have an understanding of the technical
requirements for those areas or to be assisted by someone with this knowledge.
D.7.2.2 Frequency of inspection
The interval between the LPS inspections should be determined by the following factors:

IEC 62305-3:2024 © IEC 2024 – 133 –
– the use of the protected structure, especially with regard to the consequential effects of
damage;
– the materials of the individual LPS components;
– the type of surface to which the LPS components are attached;
– the local environment:
• for a corrosive atmosphere environment the intervals between inspections should be
based on the probable rate of corrosion;
• LPSs installed in locations with significant temperature extremes should be inspected
after freezing and thawing cycles and heat extremes that can affect the materials used;
– the soil conditions:
• corrosive soils;
• earthing systems in locations where the earth freezes to the depth of the earthing system
or locations with large variations in soil moisture.
In addition to the above, an LPS should be inspected whenever any significant alteration or
repairs are made to a protected structure and following a known or suspected lightning
discharge to the LPS.
The data source in determining when an inspection, due to a lightning discharge, should be
initiated, can be from a visual observation, by a lightning strike counter, by a report of a strike
in the vicinity or recorded by a lightning location system conforming to IEC 62858 [16].
Inspection frequencies of one year for visual inspections and three years for a complete
inspection that includes electrical testing, can be selected. Where a visual inspection is not
performed at least once per year, a complete inspection should be performed every two years.
LPS systems in areas of severe weather excursions or extreme weather conditions, for example
parts of the LPS exposed to severe mechanical stresses such as flexible bonding straps in high
wind areas, SPDs on pipelines, outdoor bonding of cables, earthing systems should be visually
inspected at 6-month intervals and a complete inspection and testing should be performed once
a year. An acceptable exception to the yearly test schedule would be to perform the tests on a
14- or 15-month cycle where it is considered beneficial to conduct testing (for instance earth
testing) over different times of the year to get an indication of seasonal variations, like in areas
with extreme seasonal variations in temperature or rainfall. For such cases, it would be
acceptable to perform the visual inspections at frequencies one-half of the electrical testing so
the visual inspections can be performed at the same time as the electrical testing.
LPS systems utilized in applications involving structures with a risk of explosion should be
visually inspected at one-year intervals. Electrical testing of the installation should be performed
once a year. An acceptable exception to the yearly test schedule would be to perform the tests
on a 14- or 15-month cycle, where it is considered beneficial to conduct earth resistance testing
over different times of the year to get an indication of seasonal variations. For such cases, it
would be acceptable to perform the visual inspections at frequencies one-half of the electrical
testing so the visual inspections can be performed at the same time as the electrical testing.
Structures containing sensitive internal systems, office blocks, commercial buildings or places
where a high number of people can be present, can warrant a complete inspection (including
electrical testing) every year.
NOTE The functioning of the internal lightning protection measures, including the lightning protection equipotential
bonding, can be assessed at the same time.
An improvement of the earthing system should be considered when the measured resistance
values show larger changes in the resistance than anticipated in the design, especially when
the measured resistance increases over several successive inspections.

D.7.2.4 Visual inspections
Visual inspections should be made to ascertain whether:
– the design conforms to this document;
– there are loose connections and breaks in the LPS conductors and joints;
– any part of the system has been weakened by corrosion, especially at ground level;
– all visible earth connections are intact (functionally operational);
– all visible conductors and system components are fastened to the mounting surfaces and
components which provide mechanical protection are intact (functionally operational) and in
the right location;
– there have been any additions or alterations to the protected structure which would require
additional protection;
– there has been indication of damage to the LPS, to SPDs or any operation of the SPD
disconnector (fuse, circuit breaker) which protect SPDs;
– correct equipotential bonding has been established for any new services or additions which
have been made to the structure (interior and exterior) since the last inspection, and that
continuity tests are performed for these new additions;
– bonding conductors and connections inside the structure are present and intact (functionally
operational);
– separation distances have been established for new equipment and are maintained for
existing items;
– documentation from the last inspection has been checked to ensure tests are up to date and
any discrepancies have been resolved for bonding conductors, joints, shielding devices,
cable routing and SPDs.

– performing continuity tests, especially of those parts of the LPS which are not visible for
