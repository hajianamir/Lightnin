1. Overview
1.1 Scope
The test methods and techniques used to measure the electrical characteristics of the grounding system include
the following topics:
— Establishing safe testing conditions
— Measuring earth electrical resistivity
— Measuring the power system frequency resistance or impedance of the grounding system to remote
earth
— Measuring the transient (surge) impedance of the grounding system to remote earth
— Measuring step and touch voltages
— Verifying the integrity of the grounding system
— Reviewing common methods and procedures for performing ground testing
— Reviewing instrumentation characteristics and limitations
— Reviewing various factors that can distort test measurements
1.2 Purpose
This guide presents practical effective methods for measuring soil electrical resistivity and the condition
and performance of grounding systems. Measurable parameters include grid resistance, grounding system
impedance, ground potential rise (GPR), touch and step voltages, and current distributions in and from the
system. These grounding grids and systems typically consist of interconnected grounding components ranging
in complexity from a few ground rods to large grids with many ground rods or wells, buried conductors, and
external ground connections. External ground connections may include overhead shield/ground/neutral wires,
underground cable sheaths/neutrals, counterpoises, grid tie conductors, metallic pipes, and other connections
that provide paths to remote earth.

This guide is intended to assist the engineer or technician in obtaining and interpreting accurate, reliable data.
The factors that influence the choice of method are discussed along with a presentation of field techniques for
various types of measurements. These factors include the purpose of the measurement, the accuracy required,
the types of instruments available, the possible sources of error, and the nature of the ground or grounding
system under test. It also describes test procedures that promote the safety of personnel and property, and it
seeks to minimize operating interferences with neighboring facilities.

gate touch voltage: The gate touch voltage is the maximum potential difference between a metallic gate and

ground impedance: The vector sum of resistance and reactance between a ground electrode, grid or system to

4.1 Overview
The objective of testing a grounding system is to determine through measurement the characteristics of the
various parts of the electrical circuits that form part of or influence the performance of the grounding systems.
The detailed objectives of tests can vary depending on the stage of the grounding system life cycle. For
example, if preparing for a greenfield design (i.e., site not yet built), soil electrical resistivity testing may be
an appropriate test to gather data to support design assumptions. However, if the design is for development

such as current splits and existing component impedances. Examples of common life cycle testing stages are

— Are there any islanded grid sections (especially following site excavations)?

5.1 General safety precautions
WARNING
Serious injury may result if the following procedures are not followed when performing the tests described
in the guide. In addition to usual electrical hazards, a hazardous or potentially lethal voltage can exist
between the grounding system under test and any remote point.
A hazardous or potentially lethal voltage can exist or develop when performing the tests described in this
guide. The source of these voltages may be the test equipment itself, steady state ground current, lightning, or
a fault or other transient in the power system. Touch and step voltages around the ground electrode under test,
test equipment and remote grounds can be hazardous or potentially lethal.
A test plan should be developed and reviewed with personnel. Appropriate safety procedures shall be
developed and followed, and appropriate risk management controls shall be used. Industry electrical safety
codes and standards such as IEEE C2® (NESC®) [B2] and NFPA 70E-2012 [B74] shall be consulted. All local,

29 CFR 1910.269 [B76] applies to work on electric utility systems.

should generally be treated as though a voltage could exist between test leads and any other point. A main area
of concern involves system faults or lightning strikes, which can cause voltages as high as several thousand
volts to occur between station ground and remote points. These hazardous potentials can be transferred via
test leads and other conductive structures to create touch and step voltage hazards. It is also important to
understand the voltage produced by the test current and protect people against related potentially hazardous
touch voltages.

6. General considerations related to measurements
6.1 Complexities
The measurement of soil resistivity, grounding impedance, and potential gradient of the earth introduce
complexities not commonly encountered in other resistance, impedance, and potential measurements. Every
test lead may be coupled inductively to other current paths. This can include power system current, during
normal load or fault, that can make the test signal very difficult to measure or even cause equipment damage. It
can also include the test current itself. This requires consideration of whether the associated ac induction is an
error to be determined and corrected or can be ignored.
Similarly, any test lead may connect to an electrode, or something else, that is at a potential other than the
impressed test voltage. Unidentified buried conductive items, such as conductive pipes or poorly insulated
cable armoring, can create errors by changing what is measured. Stray currents and standing voltages, both ac
and dc, can present further error that must be considered, ascertained, and corrected as necessary.
In some situations, it might be necessary to perform several measurements via different means and compare
results carefully to correctly determine what is in error and what measurement is reliable.
With development of residential, commercial, and industrial growth adjacent to power facilities
(e.g., substations), choosing test configurations free of inductive or conductive interference may become
increasingly difficult. The presence of overhead ground wires, buried water pipes, cable sheaths, railroad
tracks, conveyor systems, and other conductive objects can all influence the electrical circuit being tested
and can introduce significant errors. It should also be noted that overhead ground wires might be insulated
either deliberately or by poor connections (with high resistance). If there is a poor connection with a voltage-
dependent resistance, very-low test voltages may produce results different from what is found if a higher test
voltage is used. Depending on what system is being tested, poor connection may cause an error that needs to be
corrected. Testing may also identify a poor connection that needs to be remediated.
Measurements can be performed prior to interconnection of external shield wires, metallic pipes, and other
external interferences, but consideration should be given to the ohmic or conductive coupling that does occur
between grounding systems even when they are not directly connected by conductors. Similarly, inductive
coupling should always be considered.
Complexities due to variation in surface material resistivity or compactness and terrain elevation changes
along the traverse can impact the accuracy of the test results.
6.2 Test electrodes
Several measurement methods described in this guide require the use of electrodes (probes) when measuring
voltage and injecting current. The impedance of the test electrodes can have a significant effect on
measurement accuracy. The impedance of a test electrode refers to the impedance of the test electrode to
the surrounding soil, not the self-impedance of the actual metallic electrode. For example, if a ground test is
performed using the two-point test method, the measurement error can be reduced if the impedance of the test
electrode is negligible with respect to the ground being tested. Conversely, for the three-point test method,
the measurement error can be reduced if the impedance of the test electrode is similar in magnitude to the
impedance of the ground under test.
Test instruments need sufficient current flowing in the test circuit to obtain accurate test measurements and
reliable test results. An awareness of the test circuit is therefore necessary because it is possible for the test
equipment to produce insufficient test current if the resistance of the test electrode is too high.

Insufficient test current may include the following cases:
a) Current lower than the instrument sensitivity
b) Current in the order of magnitude of the stray current in the earth
c) Both case a) and case b)
In case a), one corrective action available at the site of measurement is to increase test current. This can be
done either by increasing the voltage of the power supply or by decreasing the resistances of the electrodes
involved in the current circuit. Increasing the power supply voltage might not be an option due to limitations in
the measuring instrument. If an instrument with higher voltage is used, care must be taken to avoid dangerous
potentials on the electrodes and test leads.
In any of the three cases, it may be possible to use equipment more sensitive to the test current and less
sensitive to the stray current.
The resistance of the test electrode can be reduced by driving the rod deeper into the soil, pouring water
(with or without salt) around the rod, or driving additional rods and connecting them in parallel. Reducing
the resistance of test electrodes can allow higher test current that sometimes can reduce error. However, in
some scenarios, reducing the resistance of potential or current electrodes may introduce or increase errors.
Care should be taken when using these techniques. If a current electrode is made up of parallel ground rods,
then adequate spacing (not closer than the below-grade length of the current electrode) between the ground
rods can reduce the impact of mutual resistance. The maximum allowable resistance values of the current and
potential electrodes will depend on the type of instrument used.
A potential probe with high resistance to remote earth can influence the voltage measured by the instrument. If
this resistance is of the same order of magnitude as the input impedance of the instrument, then the instrument
will measure inaccurately. It will always measure only part of the actual voltage as the meter and the electrode
form a voltage divider. In cases with low soil resistivity, the error caused by this influence may be insignificant,
but in the case of high-resistivity soil, the user might consider reducing the resistance of the electrode to reduce
this error. Otherwise, if this influence prevails, then the indicated value will be less than the actual value. This
error is commonly nonconservative and can be substantial, especially in high-resistivity soils. In such cases,
lowering the test electrode resistance should be considered.
6.3 Stray direct currents
Conduction of electricity in the soil is electrolytic, and direct current results in chemical reactions and potential
differences. Direct potentials are produced between various types of soil and between soil and metal by galvanic
action. Cathodic protection systems (e.g., of pipelines or jetties), dc railroad tracks, and dc transmission lines,
wired telephone systems, and power electronics are some of the sources of dc currents in the soil. Galvanic
potentials, polarization, and stray direct currents can seriously interfere with direct current measurements.
Solar-induced telluric currents, which can be quasi-dc, can also influence test results. However, when using
periodically reversed direct current for resistance measurements, the resulting values can be accurate but may
not be a suitable value for an alternating current impedance analysis. It is common but not certain that ac tests
have good immunity to dc noise.
6.4 Stray alternating currents
Stray alternating currents in the earth, in the grounding system under test, and in test electrodes present
complications. The effects of stray alternating current can be mitigated in ground measurements by using
an ac test frequency that is not present in the stray current or by using a range of frequencies such that the
noise is a very small error. The use of filters or narrowband measuring instruments, or both, is often required

to overcome the effects of stray alternating currents. It is common but not certain that dc tests have a good
immunity to ac noise.
6.5 Reactive component of impedance of a large grounding system
While small ground grids are principally resistive, large grids can be highly reactive, especially considering
the real component of the impedance is commonly small compared to the reactive component (Harrison
[B48]). Certain precautions are necessary when measuring the power frequency (usually either 50 Hz or
60 Hz) impedance of a large grounding system. For such measurements, the test devices are operated at a
test frequency that is slightly above or below the power system frequency to obtain ac noise rejection during
the test. A frequency sweep can be used to provide information to allow the results to be corrected to the
appropriate power frequency and thereby be more accurate.
6.6 Coupling between test leads
Inductive coupling can occur between components of two or more ac circuits by means of the mutual inductance
that associates the circuits. The coupling effect between test leads becomes important when measuring low
values of ground impedance. Any voltage produced in the potential lead due to coupling from test current
flowing in the current lead (or other paths), is included in the measured voltage and needs to be assessed and
possibly corrected. The 50 Hz or 60 Hz inductive coupling between two parallel test leads might be as high as
0.1 Ω /100 m (0.1 Ω /300 ft). As a result, the error can be appreciable because low ground impedance usually
occurs in ground grids that cover large areas, and long test leads are typically required to reach remote earth.
Thus, coupling between test leads can introduce large errors when a ground grid covering a large area has a
relatively low impedance.
Conversely, a ground grid that covers a small area usually has a relatively high ground impedance, which
allows shorter test leads to reach remote earth. Thus, the effects of coupling can be expected to be worse
on measurements of large area, and low-impedance grounds. As a rule of thumb, test lead coupling is
usually negligible on measurements of grounds that are 10 Ω or greater, but it is almost always important on
measurements of 1.0 Ω or less, and can be significant in the range between 1.0 Ω and 10 Ω .
Test lead coupling can be reduced by appropriately routing the potential and current test leads. When test lead
coupling is anticipated, separating the leads or running the test leads in perpendicular directions may reduce
test lead coupling.
6.7 Interference from conductive objects
Partially or completely buried conductive objects, such as fences, rails, and metallic pipelines, located in the
vicinity of the soil, ground grid, or grounding system being tested, may have considerable influence on test
results (Dawalibi and Mukhedkar [B33], Rudenberg [B84]). Soil potential contours are distorted, and voltage
gradients are increased, when measured near conductive objects.
Whenever the presence of conductive objects is suspected in the area where measurements are to be
performed, and the location of these objects is known, the influence of these objects on the measurement
must be considered and recorded. Locate test probes as far as possible from buried structures (unless the
intent of the test is to assess these structures). Where this is not possible, aligning the test probes in a direction
perpendicular to the routing of these structures can assist in error determination and correction.

Test equipment with different probe layout configurations and units of measure may require caution during
testing and analysis. Practitioners should confirm that data points were taken with the same test method, probe

Different types of test equipment can cause issues with units of measure (e.g., Ω ⋅ m , Ω ⋅ cm , Ω ⋅ ft ), and

7. Electrical soil resistivity
7.1 Overview
Electrical soil resistivity ( ρ ) is an important component of many parameters used in assessing grounding system
designs. Since soil resistivity may vary from under 1.0 Ω ⋅ m to 109Ω ⋅ m , the performance of grounding grids
with such varying electrical parameters can be challenging to predict. The grounding specialist is tasked with
balancing accurate geological soil interpretations using well-developed geotechnical methods, with electrical
performance characteristics of grounding systems installed in varying soil conditions. Common soil electrical
resistivity test methods for grounding purposes include the Wenner, Schlumberger, Palmer, and driven rod
methods. The measured data is then used to develop electrical soil models for use in grounding grid design,
safety performance assessment, and other power system analysis (e.g., line impedance calculations).

7.3.6 Geological information and soil samples
Extensive geotechnical soil boring may be performed at sites before construction, providing soil samples

— Type of soil in each layer
— Moisture content
— Depth of water table
— Soil core resistivity values
A novel approach is to use soil or rock samples obtained from bore holes to directly measure the sample
resistivity; however, these should not be used in isolation from other soil information. This method has
challenges in maintaining the in situ soil density and moisture content and should be used with significant
caution. It is useful to obtain resistivity data from multiple test samples, noting the depths of the test samples

uncertain compaction of soil in the test cell can cause errors that should be considered in the analysis.
Note that often laboratory testing is performed with samples fully saturated for the purposes of determining
minimum resistivity; these values should not be used for grounding analysis.

7.4 Test planning
7.4.1 Instrumentation, test leads and probes
If testers are battery powered, the capacity should allow for taking multiple readings over a test day without
needing to recharge or replace batteries. Some meters may give erroneous results when used under high
humidity or winter conditions. Damaged insulation on test leads may affect the accuracy of measurements as
test current may leak into the soil along the test lead length (e.g., the jacket is punctured).
The test current is usually a square wave, often with polarity reversal, commonly in the frequency range of
70 Hz to 150 Hz, or below 10 Hz. Inductive coupling between test leads can cause errors with some instruments,
especially if the test leads are not fully unreeled. Even without coupling, coiled test leads can cause errors with
some instruments simply due to the inductance. Repeating measurements at a different signal frequency can
demonstrate that inductance and coupling errors are minimal.
Testing soil resistivity near power lines can add significant 50 Hz to 60 Hz interference to the measured
potentials on long traverses, which may cause error. Reviewing manufacturers’ specifications and/or bench
testing (with a noise source) can determine an instrument’s susceptibility to this error.
If the test instrument has a high-output voltage capability (commonly 200 V dc to 400 V dc), they are expected
to better perform in very high resistivity soils. Some meters provide a warning to the operator when the contact
resistances of current probes and/or potential probes are too high; however, other testers may have an increase
in reading errors.
Lightweight test leads (e.g., 0.8 mm2 or No. 18 AWG) may be easier to handle and large enough for the test
current from most meters. However, care should be taken as mechanical forces may cause damage during
deployment and recovery. Best practice is choosing test leads and connectors with good mechanical withstand
to suit methods and environment.
Short metallic probes can be placed quickly during the tests. Cathodic potentials due to different metals are
not an issue when using purpose-built meters. Standing dc voltages, including galvanic potentials, are usually
averaged out by the polarity reversing dc method.
When testing in high-resistivity soil, the electrode contact resistance can be reduced, and thereby the test
current can be increased, by applying water. Salt added to the water may also be of benefit (it lowers probe
contact resistance). Alternatively, driving the probes deeper into the ground, or adding multiple probes at
current locations, can lower resistance and increase the magnitude of the current signal. However, this practice
increases the probe size and apparent depth, and compensation of measurements may be required.
7.4.2 Effect of seasons and weather
Shallow soil resistivity is more likely to be affected by seasonal changes or changes in weather. For soil near
the surface, dry, rainy, and cold weather can make testing difficult (e.g., installing the test probes into the soil).
Frozen soils reduce ionization of the electrolyte in the soil and increase test probe contact resistance. Areas
with significant seasonal or water table variations can affect readings depending on what time of the year the
test is conducted. All these cases should be identified and assessed with consideration.
7.4.3 Number of traverses
A minimum of two traverses running in different directions are recommended given that the soil may contain
horizontal and vertical variation in resistivity. Several shorter and longer traverses can capture differences in
resistivity. Proximity to metallic fences, transmission lines, distribution lines, and buried metallic bodies can
lead to errors.

7.4.7 Proximity to other conductors
As traverses increase in their spacing, other conductors in the area can interfere with readings. For example, a
test parallel to a conductive fence 15 m (50 ft) away may have no substantial error when the traverse spacing
is less than 5 m (16 ft). However, as the traverse continues, the fence will act in part as a “shortcut” to the test
current and make the underlying soil resistivity value be lower than the real value. The same traverse not
parallel (but still in electrical proximity) to the fence will also lead to error, especially for the determination
of underlying layer resistivity. Other examples of conductive interference include buried or aboveground
pipelines, railway lines, buried cables with sheaths that leak current (ineffective insulted sheaths such as steel
wire armored cables with jute serving), and steel reinforced concrete.
Best practice is to investigate potential buried conductors on site (e.g., a services search). Reviewing aerial or
site photographs prior to testing may also be of value. Visual inspection once at site is also highly recommended.
Signs of possible hidden items of concern can include easement pillars, and building or demolition works.
Additionally, plotting test results as the crew carry on the tests can identify possible interference (e.g., a linear
decline in apparent resistivity can often indicate conductive interference). Ideally, traverses should be routed
in areas not containing or adjacent to buried conductors. Reciprocal measurements (reversing current and
voltage leads) as described in (7.4.5) can identify conductive interference as a significant difference in the
measured resistance values.
One study determined that errors in measured Wenner apparent resistivity values remain under 10% if the
offset distance from a station ground grid is comparable to the maximum a probe spacing used in a survey
(Blattner [B16]). However, the offset distance does not need to be larger than the size of the station.
7.5 Performing field measurements of soil resistivity
7.5.1 Safety considerations
A written job safety analysis with briefing should be conducted prior to testing. All team members should
understand the testing process, be aware of potential safety hazards, and have the authority to stop the testing
to resolve issues as they arise. Test personnel should maintain clearance from the test probes when energized
by test sets injecting more than 50 V.
Test personnel must maintain communication to stay properly coordinated. Since probes are typically installed
in the soil with hammers, Personal Protective Equipment may include eye protection and work gloves in
addition to any site-specific requirements, such as high-visibility apparel. Measurement tapes should be
nonmetallic (nonconductive). Permission to test should be obtained prior to measuring on private and public
property. Utility locates should be requested. Avoiding damage to property (e.g., crops, vegetation, roadways)
should be considered when testing.
7.5.2 Fixed or moving traverse center
The historic (and most common) approach is to conduct soil traverses around a fixed center when the spacing
is increasing between measurements. However, some practitioners move the traverse center while increasing
probe spacing (e.g., fixed end point method), which some consider a faster technique that requires only three
long test leads and one short test lead. The fixed center is the most commonly used approach.
For soil that is homogeneous, or horizontally layered, over a large area, the errors will be small. For soil that
has resistivity variations that are nonhorizontal layering, the errors can be significant, and the fixed center
technique is recommended. In the field, it is often not clear what the soil structure is, and therefore, traverses
with a moving center should be used with caution.

7.6 Interpreting soil resistivity models
7.6.1 Choosing the number of layers to interpret
A two-layer resistivity model can provide adequate accuracy when the underlying soil contains a single
interface, such as a uniform upper soil layer over a single type of rock or permeable soil above and below the
water table.
Three-layer models may be needed when either the soil or the rock have greater complexity, or when frozen
soil near the surface is considered. Three-layer models are especially necessary when the apparent resistivity
plot shows a “sandwich” layer structure, with the middle layer either higher or lower in resistivity than
the outer two layers. This is because when a soil layer resistivity changes from a low magnitude to a high
magnitude (relative to each other), current tends to stay within the low-resisitivity soil. This tends to cause
voltage gradients to extend further from an installed ground grid rather than if the soil was uniform with a
low-resistivity value. If subsequent deeper layers in the soil are of a lower resistivity, measurements of the
magnitude of the bottom layers can be difficult to assess since the high-resistivity layer will significantly affect
the measurements at larger spacings. However, the goal of characterizing the effective electrical performance
of the soil is important. Assessing a representative number of layers depends on other factors such as the
following:
a) The size of the ground grid to be installed (or is already installed) (e.g., large substation, small
distribution pad, small pipeline appurtenance, wind turbine footing),
b) The type of effects to be studied (e.g., grounding safety, electromagnetic ac interference studies,
transmission line shielding performance)
Assessing the number of layers to interpret depends on the magnitude of resistivity from one layer to the next
and can have a significant effect on a grounding grid design. In many cases, the use of one or more simplified
models can be effective and efficient.
Increasing the number of soil layers in the model can help reduce the calculated rms error. This may, however,
be a misunderstanding since a model with sufficient degrees of freedom may fit any number of data points.
Improving the curve fit by adding additional soil layers may decrease the accuracy of the calculated grounding
results when there are errors in the soil resistivity data set. It will not improve the accuracy of the calculated
electrode resistance or the touch and step voltages if the apparent resistivity data has error due to the sources
mentioned in 7.4.4.
7.6.2 Consolidating multiple traverse data sets
When multiple traverses show significant variation in resistance or resistivity values, they should be
individually interpreted. The final model could be based on a combination of the results. However, differences
may arise due to proximity to conductive objects; the need to test in less congested areas some distance away;
localized anomalies in the soil; large probe contact resistance; interference from power lines; or due to limited
resolution in the testing meter on long spacing surveys.
When multiple traverses have data points that are similar in value, the user may choose to combine the results
before interpretation to reduce the effect of soil anomalies, interferences, or instrument noise. Caution is
required.
Where testing areas local to the grid are congested and only allow short surveys (to avoid interference), longer
traverses may not be possible. It is recommended to test in a more open area near the site (judged to have
similar soil resistivity). Geological investigation and review of results from previous resistivity testing or
borehole data in both locations may help demonstrate that deep strata are similar and may have comparable
soil resistivity values.

8.1 General
The methods used to measure ground grid resistance presented in this clause have been used since the 1930s.
For many electrical grounding systems, the grid resistance (R ) provides key information in assessing the GPR.
g
The most common methods for measuring grid resistance covered in this part of the guide are the two-point,
fall-of-potential, and slope methods. The methods are a combination of data acquisition and interpretation of
the test results.
The measurement methods are principally concerned with accurately measuring the ground grid resistance of
a stand-alone ground grid where no other electrical grounding interconnections are present.
Instruments using induction coils (commonly in the form of a clamp-on ammeter) are also discussed as a
method for resistance measurement. These instruments can be used for measuring a loop resistance in a similar
way to the two-point method. They provide useful results for resistance dominated loops.
8.2 Background
8.2.1 Historical development of test methods
The need for measuring the resistance of buried grounding grids was developed shortly after the widespread
implementation of a grounded three-wire and four-wire system. Protective relays of equipment protection
systems require a low enough ground grid resistance to detect ground faults. Thus, the requirement of certain
minimum ground grid resistance values was implemented depending on the asset. Over time, the design
practice of using target grid resistance values was expanded to most electrical installations. This practice
affected system performance across power generation, transmission electrical substations, transmission lines,
and distribution substations down to distribution loads.

8.2.3 Common sources of error and instrumentation considerations
Common sources of error introduced by the grid resistance measurement methods include the following:
a) Mutual earth resistance (MER): In circumstances where the remote current electrode is not installed
beyond the influence of the ground grid under test, MER will occur between the two grids. This error
is nonconservative and not commonly detected. Analysis techniques can be used to detect and correct
these errors in most circumstances.
b) Inductive and conductive interference: Between the test circuit and a buried or aboveground
metallic infrastructure, this interference can cause error. Buried pipelines and metallic fences
can cause some of the test current to divert and flow along the pipe and fence rather than through
the soil. The result is typically the lowering of the apparent resistance compared to the case when
metallic infrastructure is not proximate or does not provide the same “shortcut” in a real scenario.
Transmission line shield wires or distribution MGN can interfere in a similar way and will typically
reduce the apparent resistance. Other infrastructure, such as large steel-reinforced concrete structures
(e.g., bridges, foundations, dams), can also significantly influence the measurement. Inductive
interference is usually not a source of error when using a dc test signal.
c) Test lead inductive interference: Between test leads, this interference can affect the measurements.
When the voltage test leads are run parallel to current test leads, the EMF induced on the voltage
measurement circuit can be a significant source of error with ac and switched dc measurements for
some instruments.
d) Loop inductance: Test lead or other circuit inductance can lead to errors in switched dc measurements
for some instruments and/or some configurations. This error relates to loop inductance large enough
to cause the transient at dc polarity reversal to extend inside the measurement window. The errors
are nonconservative. To help reduce the risk of this error, test leads should be fully unreeled during
measurements.

8.2.4 Safety considerations
Most ground grid resistance meters limit the maximum applied voltage across the injection terminals and/or
the available current. However, some instruments can produce hazardous voltages. Consult the manufacturer
documentation for all safety hazards associated with the capabilities of the instrument.
The test methods described in subsequent clauses could expose the public and test personnel to lethal voltages
if applied to energized substations. Since most methods involve extending test leads beyond a ground grid to
a remote location, direct transfer voltages on the test leads are possible when applying the test methods and a
ground fault occurs.
Another critical hazard is induced voltages and current on the test leads due to capacitive and inductive
coupling with overhead transmission lines or underground power cables. The induced voltage and current on
the test leads can be due to steady-state or fault conditions, and they can be sourced from completely different
substations. These hazards can be present while testing de-energized ground grids due to other sources of
energization not associated with the area under test. Caution is needed.
If the methods described in this guide are used on energized ground grids or near energized power sources, it is
important to assess all hazards, provide for safe work methods, and plans for managing the risk of exposure to
the public and to test personnel.
8.2.5 General theoretical value of ground resistance
Resistance measurements of a ground grid or ground electrode have been performed since the 1930s. They
still are the most common grounding test in the electric utility industry. The resistance data provides a quick
estimate of the GPR (I × R ) of the ground electrode. Substation ground grids are typically designed to limit
g g
the touch and step voltages to tolerable values. Unlike substation ground grids, transmission or distribution
pole grounds are commonly designed and installed based on limiting their resistance to remote ground to
specified values. This practice is practical and suitable for improving lightning performance of transmission
and distribution line systems. In either case, the measurement of ground grid or ground electrode resistance
can be an important part of designing or analyzing a grounding system.
Connections to earth have complex impedances that include resistive, capacitive, and inductive components,
all of which affect ground grid performance. The resistance of the connection to remote earth is of particular
interest to those applications concerned with power frequencies because it is affected by the resistivity of the
earth in the connection, assuming the grounding system is small enough that the resistance dominates the
overall ground grid impedance. The capacitance and inductance values are of interest to those applications
concerned with higher frequencies, such as radio communications and lightning systems, large grounding
systems, or interconnected grounding systems (where the resistance does not dominate the overall ground
impedance).
A substation grounding system typically consists of buried ground conductors connected to several
transmission structure and distribution pole grounds interconnected by shield wires and neutral conductors.
The interconnected impedance of this type of grounding system will be referred to as the “grounding system
impedance.” Most isolated pole grounds and small ground grids are predominately resistive. However, larger
grounding grids, especially in low soil resistivity areas, will have a significant reactive component as will
grounding systems with a significant contribution by alternative current paths (such as cable sheaths, shields,
and neutrals). This clause describes techniques for measuring the resistance of isolated grounding grids.

The resistance of a ground grid largely depends on the resistivity of the surrounding soil and the extent and
configuration of the buried electrode(s). Earth at a given location can be composed of combinations of dry soil,

other high-resistivity materials, creating significant difference in grounding performance and the associated
potential gradients and touch and step voltages. The ground grid resistance can vary between seasons as the
temperature, moisture content, and density of the soil change and physical differences in soil can make the
electrical differences even larger.
The performance of a ground grid in a given soil is dependent largely on the overall size of the ground grid and
the resistivity of the soil. The addition of conductors and ground rods within an existing ground grid system

After the installation of a substation grid or other grounded structure, the settling of the soil with annual
cyclical weather changes tends to reduce the ground resistance during the first year or two.
Calculated, estimated, or theoretical values of the resistance of an electrode to remote earth can vary from the
measured value because of the following factors:
a) Inadequacy of the analytical methods used in the estimation of the resistance.
b) Earth resistivity at the time of the measurement being different from those used in the calculations.
c) Inaccurate or insufficient extent of the earth resistivity testing used for estimation; for example,
insufficient number of traverses taken, insufficient probe spacing, and inadequacy of the
instrumentation used.
d) Presence of any buried metallic structures or other conductors (e.g., shield wire grounds) in the
proximity of the testing site.
e) Clamp-on meter readings might contain a large error if the reactance in the test circuit is significant
compared to the resistance of the test circuit due to the high-frequency test current. Other significant
errors may be introduced if the filters are inadequate to filter out 50 Hz/60 Hz frequencies caused by
stray currents. Clamp-on test meters are particularly subject to errors caused by reactance in the test
circuit with test frequencies typically ranging from 1 kHz to 4 kHz. Also, if metallic ground loops are
present, clamp-on ground meters might not measure the intended ground resistance. Some models
allow the test frequency to be adjusted to detect reactance errors and/or can indicate metallic ground
loops.
For ground grids that are influenced by the seasonal changes, it may be prudent to perform the soil resistivity
measurements during weather conditions that yield conservative values for the location (e.g., topsoil drying
out or freezing). Alternatively, the designer needs to use engineering judgment to adjust the results of a
resistivity test done in a nonideal seasonal condition to reflect the likely change that could occur in a more
extreme seasonal condition.

8.3.2 Two-point method
In the two-point method, the resistance of the subject ground electrode is measured in series with a known
well-grounded system. The resistance of the known well-grounded system is presumed to be negligible
in comparison with the resistance of the subject ground. One probe of the instrument is connected to the
subject ground and the other probe is connected to the known well-grounded system. The measured value
is the resistance to earth of the subject ground plus the known negligible ground. This measurement may
alternatively be made with a clamp-on resistance meter but with other necessary considerations. An alternative
to assuming the well-grounded system is negligible is to use a known, estimated, or otherwise measured and
then subtract that from the loop resistance.
One application of this method is to measure the resistance of a driven ground rod with respect to a nearby
residential or industrial structure. Typically, these structures have a low-resistance ground due to their ties with
the neutral conductor of the power supply system. As such, they can be considered a known well-grounded
system that can produce a test result with reasonable accuracy.
This method is subject to large errors when testing low-resistance grounds. If the subject ground electrode and
known well-grounded system are too close to each other, then the MER between the ground grids can also be a
source of error. However, this method is a useful tool where a “go” or “no-go” type of test is all that is required.

8.3.4 Grid resistance fall-of-potential method
The grid resistance fall-of-potential (FOP) method involves passing a current between a ground grid (G ) and a
current probe (C P ), also known as a “current injector electrode,” and then measuring the voltage between the
ground grid and a potential probe (P P ), as shown in Figure 10. The grid resistance is calculated by Ohm’s law
as the measured voltage divided by the current flowing through the ground grid (due to the test instrument).
To help minimize inter-electrode influences due to MER, the current probe is generally placed at a substantial
distance (D ) from the ground grid under test.

8.3.5 Simplified fall of potential (62% method, 62% rule)
Mathematical analysis in Annex C shows that an accurate result can be obtained by placing the current probe
CP at a sufficient distance D from the ground to be measured and placing the potential probe C P at 62% of
that distance. This distance is based on the theoretically correct position for measuring the exact electrode
resistance for a soil with uniform resistivity (Curdts [B28]), which assumes a sufficient distance between
the ground electrode under test and the test probes that are present, to allow test probes to be considered a
hemisphere, and further assumes that the ground electrode has no external interconnections.
To verify the above conditions are met, an initial reading is taken at 62% of the distance and then two additional
readings should be taken at 52% and 72% of the distance from the ground to be tested and the current probe.
These three readings are then compared. If both the 52% and 72% readings are within 3% of the 62% reading,
the separation was large enough, and the 62% reading can be assumed to be taken at the correct point. If the
two readings are not within 3% of the 62% reading, the current probe CP must be moved further away, and the
52%, 62%, and 72% distances recalculated, and the test repeated.

8.5 Resistance measurements by clamp-on method
The clamp-on test method measures the resistance of a grounding electrode by clamping onto the down lead
wire or the ground electrode below connected wires, as illustrated in Figure 15. During the test, the clamp-on
meter induces a voltage into the conductor in its jaw that then drives a current, which is measured, through all
available paths. The voltage-to-current ratio (resistance) is then calculated and displayed. Further information
is provided in Annex D.
When the test configuration is properly selected, it allows determination of the electrode resistance similarly to
the two-point method. The clamp-on method requires the tester to assume or estimate that the loop resistance
of the test circuit is dominated by the electrode under test and all other resistances are considered negligible in
comparison (i.e., an MGN—or shield wire—system can be so small as to be less than the measured precision).
With this assumption, the indicated reading approximates the resistance of the ground electrode (when
properly used). The second option is to separately determine or estimate the other components in the loop and
subtract those from the displayed result.
The accuracy of the clamp-on device is predicated on the proper mating and alignment of the jaws. Clamp-on
meters require cleaning of the clamp ends before use to assure proper operation. Regular testing of the meter
with a known resistance or calibration check loop can help identify any errors.
Although this method is practical and widely used for transmission and distribution lines, its theory lends itself
to some application limitations as follows:
a) The application is limited to measuring a ground electrode connected to a relatively low-resistance
grounding system in a series connection to the grounding system under test.
b) A large error can be introduced into the test measurement if the inductive reactance of the MGN
(or shield wire) is significant compared to the resistance being measured. This is particularly true of
clamp-on instruments that require high-test frequencies (e.g., greater than 1 kHz). The high frequency
injected into the test circuit will increase the reactive impedance of the circuit and can include
significant errors in the test readings if the inductance is significant. Some instruments allow lower test
frequency to be selected and, as a result, reduce the reactance errors associated with higher frequency
tests.
c) Corroded splices or connections on the neutral (or shield wire) of the system can influence the reading.
Typically, an open shield or neutral wire will be indicated by the meter as a high or infinity reading.
d) This method can be adversely affected by bonding associated with multiconnected pole grounds,
structure grounds, or any location with significant interconnecting bonds, and thus, this method is not
appropriate for substation ground grid resistance measurements. Readings must be taken at a point
where there are no connections below the point of test that connect to points above the point of test
without passing through the jaws of the instrument.
e) High-frequency noise in the system can influence the reading. A high noise-to-signal ratio can occur
during the measurement of a high-impedance ground and will be indicated on some instruments.

8.6 Resistance measurements by FOP with CTs method
A stand-alone resistance of a ground grid with multiple connections to above grade neutrals or shield wires

9. Grounding system performance measurement methods
9.1 Purpose of performance measurement
The GPR, touch voltage, step voltage, transfer voltage, and current distributions of interconnected grounding
grids are the primary electrical characteristics that are used to assess the performance of a grounding
system. Direct measurement techniques using modern current injection allow for the measurement of
grounding system performance characteristics. The measurement of the performance characteristics can be
a commissioning function to verify the safety of a grounding design, or they can be used as a tool to assess
existing interconnected and in service grounding systems.

9.2 Background of performance characteristic measurements
Historical approaches of current injection testing described in previous versions of this guide focused on
research-based assessments. Staged fault tests require significant resources and real-time data capture with
significant post-analysis. Other test methods used high-power generators injecting high current at power
frequencies. The historical approaches were rarely used to assess the performance of grounding systems on a
routine basis.
The need to routinely assess grounding systems in a cost-effective manner has been identified by asset
owners and grounding specialists. Clause 8 covers the techniques and methods for performing grid resistance
measurements. It is acknowledged that resistance alone does not accurately assess all the hazards arising in
real-world installations. New installations are often installed in proximity of existing grounded infrastructure
such as transmission overhead shield or distribution neutral conductors. Most existing installations of
grounding grids are connected to other grounded infrastructure that affect the performance of the entire
grounding system. Furthermore, as grounding systems age, as new equipment is installed or removed (e.g.,
new power lines or cables), and as surrounding infrastructure changes, the performance characteristics of the
grounding system can significantly change. Grounding connections can deteriorate and fail over time. Fault
levels can increase beyond the original design allowance. Copper theft or physical damage can lead to unsafe
conditions. For these reasons, current injection testing methods can assist asset owners in managing the life
cycle of grounding systems.

9.3.2 System analysis and design
An analytical assessment of the performance of a grounding system is made during the design phase or in

may be justified if modeling may not accurately or cost effectively be able to determine the response of the
system to a fault condition associated with a second fault path.

9.4 Current injection testing
9.4.1 Overview
Direct ground grid performance measurement using current injection testing involves the following:
a) Injection of a test signal (ac test signal at a frequency away from the fundamental power frequency)
into the grounding system over the following:
1) A transmission feeder line conductor
2) A distribution feeder line conductor
3) An overland, purpose deployed test lead conductor
4) A communications cable conductor
5) Some other conductive and ungrounded path
b) Measurements of the system response to test signal are as follows:
1) Measure GPR, touch voltage, step voltage, mesh voltage, and transfer voltage, response at key
locations
2) Measure the zone of influence for telecommunication equipment
3) Measure current distribution of all outgoing metallic connections to the grounding system under
test (current splits)
c) Evaluation of the expected performance by scaling the measured response to the value expected
during a ground fault.
CIT is distinguished from other test methods (e.g., staged fault or white noise) in that the injected test signal on
the grounding system produces a sustained voltage response at controlled levels. The test applies an ac signal
at a frequency different from the power system frequency, and the response is measured at the test frequency.
Typically, the measurement instrumentation rejects the power system frequency and other noise in the system.
The following are key characteristics of CIT:
— The frequency of the test signal is impressed at a fixed magnitude near the power frequency (typically
within 10 Hz to 20 Hz).
— Measurements of magnitude and phase angle of voltages may be performed using a frequency-
selective voltmeter. This allows for identification of hazards caused by conductive ground fault current,
inductive coupling, or a combination of both mechanisms. Suitable frequency-selective voltmeters
have high rejection ratios to interference caused by the other signals.
— Measurement of current into alternative conductive paths connected to the grounding system
(e.g., shield wires, MGN): The magnitude and phase angle of current may be recorded, which allows
for accurate vectorial analysis of current split. Measurements may also be used to identify impedance
characteristics of these current paths.
— Measurements may be obtained on a site that is de-energized or on a site that is fully energized (with
appropriate safety precautions).
— Measurements can be assessed to derive the grounding system impedance ( Z ) and grounding grid
sys
impedance ( Z ), which is commonly reported as a grid resistance (R = | Z | ).

— Measurements may be completed at two locations at the same time when utilizing the feeder CIT
method for a feeder that is electrically connected to both locations under test.

9.4.2 Types of injection circuits and instrumentation
There are two types of circuits used commonly in current injection testing: feeder and overland. Feeder
injection can be further categorized into transmission line and distribution line.
An injection circuit is composed of the following key elements:
a) A grounding system under test (in simple cases, this may be only a grounding grid)
b) A controlled voltage source injection instrument (to provide a stable test current)
c) A transmission/distribution line conductor (feeder) or an insulated test lead conductor (overland)
d) A remote electrode (e.g., grounding system, metallic structure, array of ground rods)
e) Soil and other interconnected conductive paths (e.g., shield wires, water pipes, distribution neutrals
may be part of the return path of the test current)
Measurement instrumentation and equipment used during testing may include frequency-selective meters,
flux-type current transformers (e.g., Rogowski coils), portable arresters, potential probes, test leads with reels,
and/or test clamps.

9.4.3.1 Description

conductors of a transmission line. Transmission lines as described in this guide have a system voltage over
35 kV phase-to-phase; however, various asset owners may use different voltage classifications in their

The phase conductor may be an overhead line conductor or a cable. The injected current at the ground grid
under test flows, via all available paths, to the remote test electrode, and then via the phase conductor(s)

9.4.5.1 Description

9.4.6 Instrumentation for injection sources
The following lists two main types of instrumentation equipment used in current injection testing:
a) Dedicated, frequency-selective current or voltage source
The equipment consists of a power electronic source that applies a frequency-selective low current
(commonly 1 A to 20 A).
b) AC power generator, fixed frequency
The equipment consists of a portable ac power generator operating at a different frequency from the
power system (e.g., use of a 50 Hz generator for testing a 60 Hz system). Portable ac generators provide
a constant voltage, but some may be subject to frequency drift. Test current is typically in the range of
10 A to 500 A (e.g., 240 V single-phase ac generator, 60 Hz, 120 kVA). The applied test voltage will be
limited to the voltage output of the generator, and the amount of current will be set by the test circuit
loop impedance. This often means the test power is much lower than the generator’s rating.
Historically, power generators were the only available option. The available range of dedicated electronics-
based instruments has seen them become a significantly more common choice over the last decade. Benefits
include not having to carry or manage fuel, no or lower maintenance, less bulk and weight to transport and
carry, less noise, no fumes, or hot exhausts. It is also possible with electronics-based instruments to have
a very strict frequency and stable phase that allows very narrow filtering and reliable phase measurement.
Frequency-selectable volt and amp meters used with a generator need to filter outside a wide enough band to
ensure frequency drift in the generator does not affect measurements.
9.5 Planning and setup
9.5.1 Hazards and safety
9.5.1.1 Personnel
Safety of personnel shall be considered when measuring the performance characteristics of a grounding grid.
Whether the test is performed using feeder or overland injection methods, additional hazards may be present,
which require appropriate engineering controls and safe work procedures.
CIT methods are best performed by experienced and qualified personnel, who understand the test performance
requirements and associated hazards. Although commercial equipment manufacturers may outline some of
the hazards that can be encountered, performance measurements on in-service grounding systems also have
additional risks, which shall be evaluated and mitigated for each test site.
Feeder injection methods require an outage on a transmission line or a distribution line and inserting the test
equipment in series with the circuit. Safe practices shall be established to help protect personnel from contact
with hazardous voltages.
Site hazard assessments are recommended prior to testing to determine whether appropriate systems and
processes are in place to be able to conduct the test.
Hazards such as broken or islanded underground grounding systems and copper theft can pose significant
safety hazards, and they may adversely impact the test result. It is important to determine the overall integrity
of the system prior to performing a current injection test.
Testing on in-service grounding systems is best performed by engineers and technicians who are knowledgeable
and experienced in applying engineering controls for reducing risk when working on grounding systems, and
for assessing work practices.

9.5.1.2 Induction hazards
Sources of ac induction, either capacitive or magnetic, may be present during current injection testing. In
particular, the injection circuit will often be in proximity to other energized transmission or distribution lines.
The induced voltage and current on the test circuit may pose hazards requiring engineering controls. Overland
testing methods may have significant ac induction hazards due to proximity with other energized sources.
Opening a feeder injection circuit where ac induction is present may lead to a high voltage being present across
the opening. This risk should be managed through engineering and/or procedural controls.
It is often observed that the magnitude of magnetic induction changes over the course of a CIT. In general,
induction levels will change with the magnitude of load current of adjacent power lines. Phase conductors may
have different levels of induction in a feeder test, based on the configuration of the lines being used.
9.5.1.3 Instrumentation considerations
Current injection instruments are commercially available. They are specifically designed to perform current
injection testing methods. Instrumentation has certain capabilities (ratings) and limitations, and these may
vary when used on in-service grounding systems and when used for feeder injection tests.
Since CIT requires connection of injection equipment in series with de-energized feeders, the equipment may
be exposed to power frequency induction, harmonic frequency current, and dc current noise. It is important
that the level of ac induction not cause damage to the test equipment. The level of ac induced current may be
measured with a clamp-on ammeter when the feeder is grounded. It is important to consult the equipment
manufacturer’s documentation for application guidelines. The goal is to determine the maximum induced
current that can be present inside the equipment to remain undamaged and/or remain in operation.

— Maximum output power
— Maximum output voltage
— Maximum output current
— Frequency range of injected current

The test environment and test circuit need to be considered when specifying and selecting test equipment. The
parameters to consider include range of soil resistivity and grounding system impedances, and consideration
of the test loop impedance. The test loop impedance comprises the grounding grid impedance, the source
internal impedance, the test lead or feeder impedance, the remote electrode impedance, soil resistivity, and any
other interconnected paths.
The maximum output voltage of the equipment and the loop impedance of the test circuit produce test current.
However, if the current exceeds the equipment’s maximum current, a lower voltage will commonly be applied
such that the maximum current is set (if not, circuit protection devices may operate). Similarly, if the current
times the applied voltage exceeds the equipment output power rating, then less of both (V, I) need to be applied
to the test circuit. Ideally, CIT equipment should manage this control automatically.
Where ac induction causes current flow in the test circuit, the CIT equipment may have different performance
depending on current magnitude. For example, the maximum current that can be injected may be the
equipment’s maximum output current less the induced current present.
9.5.2 Feeder injection setup and planning
The choice of a suitable transmission or distribution line to use for a feeder injection depends on many factors,
such as follows:
a) AC induction: Expected level of current on the proposed feeder used for the test may be high.
b) Line fault contribution to the system: Using feeders with larger fault current contribution may
provide the most accurate simulation; however, exceptions arise, and proper consideration should be
given to feeder selection.
c) Protection clearing time: Lines with longer clearing times may be the worst case for touch and step
voltage hazards; simulating the performance of the given line under real fault conditions is valuable.
d) Availability of noncritical lines: Account for limitations on which lines are available for outages.
e) Location of injection point within a grounding grid: For large grids, the placement of the current
injection point greatly affects measurement results. Its position must be determined based on worst-
case scenario for hazards or total risk.
f) Length of the feeder: It influences the magnitude of the circuit impedance and of the test current.
g) Conservative or optimistic inductive performance: In some cases, verifying inductive performance
takes precedence over GPR or voltage response characteristics (e.g., underground cable networks).
Selecting which transmission line to use for the test is often limited by the electric utility and the availability of
planned outages. An outage on a line used for this type of testing will fall under established procedures within
the transmission line owner or transmission system operator (TSO).
It may be preferable to use a distribution feeder over a transmission feeder to assess a site where distribution
protection clearing times are longer. It is recommended that practitioners consider the duration and worst-case
earth-return fault current for all ground fault scenarios (when multiple voltage levels are involved at a site).
Setting up an injection circuit using feeders involves both experienced test personnel and utility staff. The
utility staff usually has a permit to isolate and ground the system (via a ground switch or TPG, called the
master ground) from all potential sources of supply. It is common to request, often as part of the permit
application, for additional TPG to be installed at the location where the CIT equipment is inserted into the
test circuit. It is also necessary to be able to either open the circuit between the two grounding points or to
disconnect the master grounds. The additional TPG, which may be called the test TPG in some countries, are
to facilitate the connection of the CIT equipment into the circuit and will not be acting as protective grounds
once reconfigured. See IEEE Std 1048 and IEEE Std 1246.

It is recommended to verify that the integrity of all ground connection points to the main grounding grid is

If induction current on the line phases is to be measured, the test TPG must be connected to each phase

of the CIT equipment should be connected before this. Any test TPG cables from phases that are not going to
form part of the injection circuit should be insulated, isolated, or otherwise treated against possible hazardous
voltages arising from the line. The correct configuration and operation of the CIT equipment is essential, and
manufacturer provided information will be necessary.
Once the test circuit is configured to the manufacturer’s instruction and the test TPG are all properly treated,
the master ground at the CIT equipment location should be removed. This allows the injected current to flow
through the soil to the remotely grounded point or points without any local close circulation.
All performance characteristics such as current splits, surface voltage traverses (e.g., fall-of-potential
traverse), touch voltage, step voltage, mesh voltage, and transfer voltage measurements can be performed
with a continuous test current flow. The performance measurements may take from a few hours to several days
depending on the scope of the assessment and the size of the site.
The CIT equipment must be removed from the test configuration in reverse order. De-energize the CIT
instrument, reground the line via the original master ground such that it is in parallel with the injection
instrument and the test TPG, disconnect both sides of the injection instrument using appropriate safety
precautions, and return the feeder to service per typical utility practices.
It should be noted that while the feeder CIT process may not be common for some utilities, it is similar in
steps, circuits, and controls to a lot of other electrical testing (e.g., protection primary injection testing, line
impedance testing). This may be useful when introducing this test method within existing company testing
programs.
9.5.3 Overland injection setup and planning
Overland injection testing may require significant preplanning due to several factors, such as follows:
a) Access to surrounding property, free of conductive or interfering infrastructure, is often difficult to
obtain.
b) Installation of a remote electrode or the connection to existing conductive infrastructure may be
time-consuming.
c) The public, as well as other company personnel, may have physical access to the injection circuit
conductor, which may require additional hazard or risk controls.
d) Terrain in remote areas can impede access, and testing may take significant time.
e) Permits to access road crossings, and private property permissions to access land, could require long-
term planning and may cause delays.
f) Correctly identifying underground assets (e.g., pipelines) may be required to determine whether
significant induction or proximity to the return electrode may be of concern.
g) The overland injection test lead may need to extend to a great distance to make MER negligible. It may
be more practicable to use a shorter lead and accept some MER that can be compensated for.

9.5.4 Measuring and evaluating voltages
The injection of test current into the grounding system produces a voltage response that is measured in many
locations using a frequency-selective voltmeter and test probes, or by measuring between different metallic
objects in and around the areas under test.
Voltage traverses may be performed to determine surface voltage gradients. Direct measurement of touch
voltage, step voltage, mesh voltage, and transfer voltage is commonly performed inside and outside the
grounding grid (e.g., at gates, fences, control cabinets, nearby pipes). These are a very direct assessment of
the hazard, independent of estimated or measured current distribution. Voltage differences across isolated or
connected metallic infrastructure are also measured to better understand the performance of the entire system.
Measuring voltages with phase can provide further insight. For example, a phase angle can show what is
causing or transferring a particular voltage and increasing or decreasing phase can indicate the presence of
inductive coupling.
9.5.4.1 Measurement of a voltage traverse
A voltage traverse is a set of consecutive measurement points of voltages along the surface of the soil, typically
along a straight line. The measurements are performed with reference to the grounding grid under test and
usually measured from the CIT ground injection point. They may include voltage traverses across an existing
grid to determine mesh voltage and possibly detect irregularities. Short voltage traverses may be performed
off the corners of the ground grid to measure the touch and step voltage; corners are often the worst-case
hazard. Voltage traverses may be extended for hundreds of meters to kilometers away from the site as required
for the assessment.

electrode attached to the end of a test lead. The “reference potential” lead is typically connected to the
grounding system.
Surfaces with aggregate can have measurements made to the surface using a watered plate. An alternative is to
use a probe that can penetrate through the surface aggregate to measure the voltage at the soil below it.
9.5.4.1.2 Measurement of resistance or impedance
Small, noninterconnected, grounding grids, especially in high-resistivity soil, are primarily resistive. Large
grounding grids (e.g., with diagonal of 200 m or greater depending on soil) may exhibit a significant reactive
component due to self and mutual inductance of the buried ground conductors. Grounding systems with
interconnections to other grounded infrastructure are even more clearly not resistive. Grounding systems that
are interconnected to distribution neutral conductors and/or overhead shield wires can have a large reactive
component, made up of series reactance, shunt capacitance, and possibly mutual impedances. The phase angle
between applied voltage and conducted current is evidence of this impedance. In a real or simulated fault, the
more significant phase angle is between the injected or faulted current and the current returned in each metallic
path.
9.5.4.1.3 External voltage traverse plot
The primary purpose for one or more long external voltage traverses is to assess the GPR of the site.
The test crew may use a probe and voltmeter to take voltage measurements with respect to the CIT injection
point along the traverse. These measurements present an increase in voltage as measurements are from
full GPR to the potential of the soil where the measurement is being taken. The measurements are literally
measuring the fall-in-potential; hence, it is often called a fall-of-potential traverse, run, or test. Measurement
spacings should increase with distance from the ground grid as the expected response is approximately a curve
of the reciprocal of the distance (1/distance). Consideration should be given as to how far the voltage traverse
should extend (toward remote earth). Ideally, the voltage traverse would continue until three consecutive
readings have a negligible change in magnitude (the plot becomes asymptotic). However, that may not be
possible or practical, and analysis methods may be needed to estimate the increase in voltage to remote earth.

9.5.4.2 Measurement of touch voltage
Touch voltage and mesh voltage are important factors in evaluating electrical safety, requiring careful
assessment through various measurement techniques. Instrumentation and measurement procedures, such as
prospective and loaded voltage measurements, are essential to ensure accurate voltage readings in different
and complex electrical installations.
Proper measurement locations and the use of appropriate tools are necessary to assess voltage conditions
effectively. Adhering to established standards and protocols ensures the safety and reliability of these
assessments across a range of scenarios.
9.5.4.2.1 Touch voltage
Touch voltage in general is any location where a person may be exposed to two different potentials between
a hand and some other part of their body. Commonly recognized touch voltage scenarios include hand-to-
hand, hand-to-feet, and hands-to-feet. Almost any contact scenario is possible, but it is usually impractical
to assess against unlikely scenarios. The most assessed are hand-to-hand and hand-to-feet, with the latter
commonly simply called a touch voltage. IEEE Std 80 provides guidance on determining safety criteria for
these scenarios.
A touch voltage is commonly measured between an accessible conductive structure and the surface on which
a person could stand 1 m distance (e.g., arm’s reach). Examples are a person operating an outdoor switch
mechanism and a person opening an equipment cabinet. Some areas of specific attention for measurement
are at fences and gates around the facility. For a grounded fence, the maximum voltage usually occurs outside
the fence at a corner farthest from the center of the grid, but this may well change where there is full or partial
fence grounding segregation. For a touch voltage at a gate, the maximum potential difference will likely occur
when the gate is in an open position.

9.5.4.2.2 Mesh voltage
The mesh voltage refers to the greatest potential difference within a site, typically the potential difference
between the full GPR and a central point in one of the larger grid squares near the station’s perimeter. However,
the location and value can vary, and measurements may need to be taken at multiple locations to assess the
mesh voltage. While the mesh voltage might not necessarily pose a hazard, it represents the largest available
voltage (assuming the grid has good integrity) that could be present in a transferred touch voltage scenario
within the ground grid.
A typical scenario where a mesh voltage becomes a touch voltage is when personnel are performing
maintenance or construction (e.g., power tool internal grounding might bring a grounded point to areas where
normal exposure to grounded infrastructure is not available).
9.5.4.2.3 Instrumentation and test probes
Voltage measurements can be performed using a frequency-selective voltmeter. The measurement of surface
voltage can be performed with either a probe or a metallic plate. Metallic plates should simulate a “foot
contact” per IEEE Std 80. Where measurements are taken with metallic plates, they should be watered to help
ensure a good connection (low contact resistance).
When measuring voltage inside a grounding grid, the test probe may be inserted through the top of the surface
rock a short distance into the subsurface soil. The test probe requires a sufficient connection with the soil to
acquire a stable voltage measurement.
For connections to metallic structures, it is also common to use alligator-style clamps or classic shrouded
meter probes.
9.5.4.2.4 Prospective voltage measurement
Prospective touch voltage (unloaded touch voltage) is the potential difference between two locations without
a person in the touch voltage situation. The prospective configuration does not include the resistance of the
body per IEEE Std 80. Touch and step voltage measurements taken with a high-input impedance meter are
prospective measurements of the voltage. All mesh voltages should be measured as prospective. Transfer
voltages measured as prospective are reliable (using loaded measurement for transfer voltages requires careful
consideration, as discussed in (9.5.4.2.5).
9.5.4.2.5 Loaded voltage measurement
A loaded touch voltage is the potential difference between two locations with a person in the touch voltage
situation (closed circuit). The presence of the individual allows current to flow through the body, which in turn
lowers the available voltage due to the drop in effective source impedance. A loaded voltage measurement is
performed with the same procedure as a prospective touch voltage measurement, but with a resistor placed
in parallel with the meter. A value of 1.0 kΩ is commonly used to represent the IEEE 80 presented body
impedance (although other resistance values are acceptable too). Some frequency-selective voltmeters have
an option to “load” the measurement, which adds the resistor internally. Manufacturer’s instructions should be
consulted to determine the maximum voltage that they can be safely loaded.
In scenarios such as fence transfer voltage, a fence can measure a high prospective voltage, but the loaded
voltage may collapse to a low value. In other scenarios, the loaded voltage may remain relatively consistent
with prospective voltage measurements.
Care should be taken relying on loaded measurements for safety compliance. An example of note is the
measurement of loaded touch voltages to a fence with corrosion. At the test voltage corrosion products can be
a high resistance and cause a large reduction in touch voltage. During a fault, the higher voltage applied will
break down many of these and result in a higher loaded voltage.

9.5.4.4 Measurement of transfer voltage
A transfer voltage is where conductive structures carry a potential to a location where the soil potential is
different. Transfer can occur as GPR or part of GPR away from the ground grid but also as transfer of a
potential closer to remote earth closer to the grid. These are commonly called transfer-out and transfer-
in. Transfer voltages can present touch voltages, but can also create soil potential that may be a threat to
other infrastructure, such as telecommunications equipment or pipelines. Common objects that may transfer
potentials are lines, fences, pipelines, railroads, and communication cables.
A transfer voltage measurement is performed with a test lead connected to a reference point of the ground
grid (normally the injection point) and the other test lead connected to a conductive object to test. The other
conductive object may be inside the grounding grid or outside the grounding grid.
Touch voltage scenarios that can arise due to these transferred potentials are also commonly measured
while performing transfer voltage measurements. These touch voltage measurements would investigate the
conductive object under test with respect to the surrounding soil and also any other conductive objects that
may be within reach. The resulting touch voltages would then commonly be compared to the appropriate
safety criteria.
The following are some examples of transfer voltage scenarios:
a) An extended security fence to a site: Voltage will transfer to the fence, allowing for touch voltage to
occur along the fence. This voltage will usually be at its worst if the fence is bonded to the grounding
system.
b) Water or gas pipes: Some facilities may have water or gas service, which may bring in remote voltage
hazards or transfer out voltage hazards.
c) A railway entering a site (that may have been used to transport heavy equipment): Since tracks
have a very low longitudinal impedance and often a much higher impedance to ground (a shunt
impedance), these most commonly transfer in a low potential with respect to the GPR. The opposite is,
however, possible.
d) Nearby pipelines: Pipelines routed to or near the site can provide a transfer mechanism.
e) Telecommunications equipment and cabling: The surface voltages can be elevated during ground
fault conditions due to remote ground connections or transferring a remote soil potential to a nearby
telephone pedestal.
f) Sidewalk with conductive reinforcing: Remote voltage may transfer in or out of the site in
urban environments. The moisture of the concrete near the reinforcing can significantly affect the
performance of nearby sidewalks from a step voltage perspective.
Measuring both transfer voltage to a nearby structure or ground, along with the touch voltage at the structure,
helps identify the source (such as transfer in or transfer out) and aids in assessing hazards. Additionally,
measuring the phase can help detect the presence of inductive mechanisms.

9.5.6 Interpretation of performance measurement data
An advantage of the CIT is the capability to measure large, interconnected energized grounding systems.
The test can accurately simulate a scaled version of the performance of the grounding system for the scenario
tested. In practice, the scenarios available for testing do not always equate to the worst-case scenarios. In some
cases, it is necessary to carry out some analysis to confirm the results are conservative compared to the true
case. In some cases, more detailed analysis is justified to determine the expected performance of the true case
more precisely.
In practice, CIT requires the collection of many data points. On-site preliminary analysis of the test data
may assist test personnel with gathering sufficient test measurements. This may include plotting voltage
traverses in the field to assess whether the measurements have extended far enough. Summing all current
split contributions (ideally vectorially) in the field is helpful to account for all out-going paths. Scaling of
measurements to expected worst-case earth-return ground fault conditions can be used to estimate expected
hazard levels on site, which may assist in any further measurements to make.
9.5.6.1 Compensation of test signal frequency
Since the injected test signal is impressed at a frequency away from the actual fault frequency, correction may
be valuable, especially for highly reactive systems. All inductive and capacitive effects are dependent on the
test frequency. Thus, voltage and current measurements may appear larger or smaller than what is expected at
the fundamental frequency, and correction to the power frequency should be considered.
The “interpolation method” involves taking transfer measurements to the same remote location for several
reconfigured injection current frequencies. The current must also be measured at each frequency, and the
transfer voltage divided by the injected current gives an apparent impedance at each frequency. The impedance
may not be the correct value as the test crew did not correct the transfer measurement to full GPR. Nevertheless,
the ratio between the impedances allows a linear interpretation to the actual power frequency, and the ratio of
this to the main test frequency allows a correction for the actual impedance, GPR and all other test voltages.

b) Impedance mismatch of two parallel transformers within a grounding grid
c) High-voltage auto-transformers installed as single-phase units may have significant currents in the
grounding grid.
d) DC current from pipeline corrosion mitigation systems, and light rail transit systems
e) Harmonics produced by power electronics in industrial plants or solar farms
f) Telluric currents (e.g., geomagnetically induced)
g) Induced currents in HV shield wires may flow into the station ground grid
Most commercially available CIT meters include a frequency-selective filter that rejects ac noise. In areas with

the noise rejection capabilities of the instrument.

the test current. Manufacturer’s documentation should be consulted to determine the potential harm or error
their instrument may suffer in these circumstances.

9.6 Staged fault test
Current injection with full fault current magnitude provides the most accurate step, touch, and transfer voltage
data. However, this type of test is rarely performed due to safety considerations, high cost, availability of
system outages, and the possibility of equipment damage.
Staged fault tests could be applied as part of tests performed by other engineering disciplines (e.g., protection).
The following are examples of test scenarios where a staged test could be applied:
a) Direct measurement of transmission line parameters: This test is performed for system protection,
operation dispatch, and planning. During a zero-sequence impedance test, the phase conductors are
grounded in one line end, and a strong single-phase current source is applied to the other end. Current
range is typically a few hundred to a few thousand amperes.
b) Protection scheme performance test: This test is performed by system protection departments for
measuring the performance of relay scheme settings and total clearing times. Real single-line-to-
ground faults are created by means of either shooting grounded wires to live lines, by closing ground
switches, or by energizing a line with a thin wire or TPG connected to earth. The power system acts as
the source and the resulting current is hundreds to thousands of amperes, depending on the equivalent
impedance of the power system at the point of fault.
c) Real power system fault analysis: Some work has been done to install voltage and current
measurement equipment at substations and record actual GPR and fault current values produced by
real feeder faults (Woodhouse et al. [B98]). For example, during a ground fault on the low side of a
delta-grounded-wye transformer, the transformer becomes an adequate source galvanically isolated
from the transmission system and the GPR can be measured.

9.8 Measurement methods using numerical compensation techniques
Some methods were developed as described in previous versions of this standard that employ the use of
numerical computation techniques. These techniques rely on specific field measurement values of voltage in
and around the grounding grid. The measured values are used to tune a grounding model used for estimating
the expected performance characteristics. This may be performed by a user changing modeling parameters, or
programmatically using dedicated software and test instrumentation.
One method involves instrumentation equipment that communicates with computer-based finite element
software to both control the test equipment and provide electrical performance characteristics (Meliopoulos
et al. [B71], [B72]). The development of a grounding grid model with this method could be performed
by personnel prior to testing (or during the test). The grounding model is used to predict the performance
measurements of the site based on test lead deployment and probe locations specified by the manufacturer. The
instrument can modify various computation parameters so the measurement inputs and the modeled results
converge. The following are several characteristics of this method:
a) The method requires a grounding model to estimate the performance characteristics. Differences
between physical installations and what are included in grounding models may lead to significant
deviations in grounding performance characteristics.
b) Some of the numerical computation methodologies may not include important effects such as mutual
inductive effects between finite elements in the model or MER effects with surrounding grounded
infrastructure.
c) Computation methods may not simulate or account for test lead mutual inductive effects with below
ground current carrying ground conductors.
d) The method assumes certain aspects of the performance characteristics of the grid such as the reactive
portion of a remote voltage profile is entirely provided by the reactance of incoming connections to the
ground grid.
e) The method assumes the “slope” method for interpreting soil and voltage response performance and
does not necessarily extend the voltage profile out of the zone of influence of the ground system.

10. Integrity testing of grounding systems
10.1 Purpose
A key reason for having a properly designed, installed, and maintained grounding system, is to help reduce the
risk of shock hazards and abnormal operating events that can arise due to fault currents, and other transients.
It is common to focus on calculating GPR, touch, and step voltages, or measuring the grid resistance or
impedance to remote ground. However, a broken or deteriorated ground connection can result in hazards to
personnel due to touch voltages many times higher than measured or estimated with a computer program,
even as high as phase-to-ground voltage (Figure 30). Similarly, such failures are a significant risk in terms of
damage to equipment and reliable supply, either through maloperation or equipment damage.
Grounding system integrity testing is commonly performed to validate a newly installed or modified grounding
system, and periodically thereafter to reconfirm its condition. Events, such as a ground fault or maloperation,
may also be reason to reassess the grounding system integrity.
The purpose of integrity testing is to validate the proper interconnection of the grounding system. Proper
interconnection includes the following:
— All things that should be bonded are properly bonded
— All things that should not be bonded are properly segregated
While some integrity testing methods use higher current levels, all common current levels are well below the
current level that tests the strength of the conductors or connectors. If strength testing is desired, higher current,
typically thousands or tens of thousands of amps, is required. If performed on an in-service grounding system
it may cause harm or damage, including annealing or partial failure of conductors or connectors. Strength
testing is most applied to samples as a type test rather than to installed grounding systems.

10.2 Principles
To verify there is a low-resistance path between points that should be bonded, all accessible grounded
structures and ground connections can be inspected and tested for integrity. Where test results indicate a
resistance greater than the expected value, conductors, and connections, including buried elements, need to be
further inspected.
Each integrity test method involves a current source and a voltmeter or ohmmeter. It is important to match the
sensitivity of the voltmeter or ohmmeter to the test current and possible ac and dc noise in the test environment.
Proper selection of this metering allows accurate measurements at any level of test current.
Integrity testing is best performed by qualified personnel familiar with grounding system hazards and power
systems equipment. A broken grounding system increases the hazards to personnel and equipment. Grounding
connections do not need to be disconnected to evaluate their effectiveness or the effectiveness of other
grounding system components.
10.3 Detection of failures or inadequacies
Assessment of a grounding system condition over the life of the substation requires an understanding of the
likelihood and consequences of possible failures. The following is a list of common failures for which integrity
testing, together with a quality visual inspection, is helpful in detecting and determining corrective action:
— Error during the installation
— Theft
— Corrosion
— Vandalism
— Mechanical damage
— Electrical damage
— Configuration change (inside or outside)
— Encroachment by the built environment
10.4 Sources of error
Integrity testing technical challenges include the ability of the testing system to accurately measure the desired
parameter in a substation or plant with very high electromagnetic interference. The interference can include
inductively, capacitively, and conductively coupled noise. Testing systems should be effective in negating the
effects of direct and indirect sources of ac and dc voltages and currents on grounding systems under test.
10.5 Method elements
Most integrity testing systems consist of similar elements: a power source, electronic metering, and test leads.
The practical challenges of performing integrity tests include portability of test meter, ease of meter use,
and test lead deployment and retrieval. The technical challenges include measurement error (which can be
compounding), especially in in-service grounding systems. It is important on both a practical and a technical
basis to understand the issues and trade-offs when selecting testing systems.

10.5.1 Portability of test set
Each time the instrument or reference point of measurement is required to be moved, the total test time
increases. High current meters often require 110 V ac to 220 V ac sources. Options for serving this load include
portable generators and station service. Larger test currents require larger and heavier leads that limit the
practical lead length and require the meter to be moved more often. Battery powered meters provide portability,
flexibility, and faster deployment. Smaller test currents allow smaller cross-section leads, which supports
longer leads, and this allows a common reference and faster testing. However, proper correction or elimination
of electromagnetic errors on longer test leads is necessary. This correction can be performed manually or
incorporated in the hardware and software of the meters. More portable instruments or those using long leads
do not usually allow higher current tests.
10.5.2 Two-wire versus four-wire systems
As the names suggest, these test configurations inject a test current between two points on the grounding
system or grid through either two-wire test leads or four-wire test leads (Figure 31). In a two-wire system, the
test current and voltage measurement share the same two leads. The test current creates a voltage drop across
the test leads that needs to be accounted for when determining the resistance or impedance of the test circuit.
In a four-wire system, the test current and voltage measurements are performed using different test leads,
eliminating the need to account for the direct effect of the test current on the voltage measurement lead.
Additionally, ac current and dc transients in the current lead induce EMF in the voltage test lead that needs to
be considered and possibly accounted for.

10.5.3 AC versus dc signals
Procedures requiring measurement correction due to effects of induced voltages on leads take extra time.
Integrity testing systems are required to either provide a large enough signal to make errors due to the power
system noise (and possibly other sources) negligible by comparison or to use other means, such as filtering and
signal type and polarity to adequately reject errors due to noise.
Energized power system equipment and conductors produce high levels of electric and magnetic fields at
fundamental power frequency and the associated harmonics. These, in turn, cause voltages and currents on the
instruments and leads used by all integrity testing systems. Measurements using ac signals may be affected
significantly by these voltages and currents, especially if at power system frequency, or another frequency
where noise is present. The length of leads for instruments using ac test signals are often restricted to limit
98


IEEE Std 81™-2025
IEEE Guide for Measuring Earth Resistivity, Ground Impedance, and Earth Surface Potentials of a Grounding System
measurement errors due to induced currents and voltages. Other instruments may use filtering to reduce
measurement errors due to induced voltages.
Testing systems using dc test signals can provide additional error rejection for all forms of ac noise. However,
standing dc voltages on the grounding system can cause measurement errors by offsetting measured voltages.
Multiple measurements using reversing polarity signals can be taken and evaluated to reject the influence of
standing dc noise.
Electric fields can also induce voltages on instruments and leads. Proximity to high-voltage buses and breaker
leads are common sources of these voltages. Intentional routing of leads can reduce the induced voltages.
Longer test leads may increase the probability of measurement and reliability issues.
Measurement systems should provide data to the user indicating the accuracy of measurement to allow for
proper evaluation of circuit under test. Consideration of common failure modes of the full meter test circuit
should be given. These include permanent and intermittent breaks in test leads, operating condition of the
power source, and standing voltages that are too large for proper measurement.
10.5.4 High current versus low current
The magnitude and type of test current varies with testing systems and instruments. It is important to
match the sensitivity of the voltmeter or ohmmeter to the test current and possible ac and dc noise in the
test environment. Where significant improvements are achievable in the sensitivity and noise rejection of
instruments (particularly the metering), accurate measurements can be achieved with significantly lower
magnitudes of test current.
10.5.5 Moving versus common reference point
There are commonly two approaches to integrity testing a grounding grid and its accessible connections, as
follows:
a) Measuring the resistance across a single joint or a small number of joints, and moving around the site
measuring additional, often series connected, joints. This method provides good resolution across
individual joints but may require current measurements to determine if low readings are due to parallel
paths. Regularly relocating the instrument can be time consuming.
b) Measuring the resistance to many points from a common (well chosen) reference point, changing to
a new reference point only if the maximum test spacing of the instrument is reached. This method
supports the detection of irregular resistance increase across the site. Less instrument relocation can
see testing completed faster.
When a test requires movement of a reference point during testing, it is best practice to measure a couple of
structures/equipment captured from the previous reference point. The addition of these couple of overlapping
measurements will help avoid the possibility of an islanded section of the ground grid being missed.
10.5.6 Identifying disconnection versus deterioration
In most integrity testing methods, a simple pass or fail resistance can be nominated and measured against to
determine if disconnection has occurred. For example, more than 1.0 Ω is clearly not locally conductively
bonded. If instruments provide adequate accuracy, a lower target resistance can be used to identify “failed”
connections requiring remediation (usually based on grid size, density, and conductor size). A simple pass
or fail target might be as low as 20 mΩ across an entire grid for some sites or 2 mΩ to 5 mΩ across a single
connector. Such low target resistances may assist in identifying conductors or connectors that are deteriorated
but not yet disconnected. However, false negatives are possible depending on the method and instrument used.

10.5.8 Sample connections versus measuring all connections
Consideration should be given to the decision whether to test only a limited number of points within a
grounding system or grid, or to test every connected point. If a sample of limited points identifies disconnection
or deterioration, then further testing is likely to find additional failures within the grounding system. Testing
all points would commonly be undertaken before performance testing or a full remediation is undertaken.
If sample of limited points within a test does not identify any issues, there is still a chance that failures may
be present within a grounding system. The greater the number of points tested, the lower the chance of an
undetected failure being present.
10.6 Common methods
10.6.1 AC two-wire test method
A typical two-wire test instrument consists of a variable source, voltage, and current measuring devices, and
two test leads. One test lead is connected to a reference ground point, and the second lead is attached to a
different ground riser or grounded structure. The test consists of passing a current between the connected risers
and measuring the voltage drop across the circuit, including the test leads. The output voltage divided by the
output current gives the impedance between the two terminals at the instrument. To determine the impedance
between the test points, the impedance of the test leads must be subtracted. This measurement is performed by
shorting the leads of the test and measuring the test set voltage while injecting test current in the shorted leads.
This measurement yields the series impedance of the test leads.
It is important to avoid loops or coils in the test leads, as this can produce significant inductance and alter the
voltage drop. One way to evaluate a ground grid is to compare the voltage drops or impedances with each
other and identify the test risers that have abnormally high impedance values. Unless measuring the phase
angle of the test lead measurement and the complete test circuit measurement, measurements are simplified to
impedance magnitudes.
Another method of applying the two-wire ac method includes measuring the current flowing toward the grid
and away from the grid using a clamp-on ammeter. The comparison of the current division can indicate if
there is a high resistance or open path in either direction. More information on this method can be found in
Gill [B44].
Keeping the reference riser connected, the second test lead is moved around to test risers at other equipment as
far as the test leads will allow. The reference riser is then moved to allow more risers to be tested. This process
continues until the entire substation ground grid is tested.
10.6.2 AC four-wire method
This test method is essentially the same as described in 10.6.1 except the voltage between the tested risers is
measured between the test points using two additional wires and a high-impedance voltmeter. This method
reduces some of the errors associated with two-wire methods (lead impedance errors and variations). The test
system commonly injects 60 Hz (or 50 Hz when nominal) current between two test points. The impedance
between those points can then be estimated. Possible errors include the effect of induction and capacitive
coupling on the test leads. The apparent impedance between different measurements is compared to determine
possible broken conductors and high-resistance connections.
10.6.3 DC four-wire method
This test method uses four test wires, two for passing current and two for measuring the voltage. Using dc
allows the direct measurement of the resistance between the two test points, or the test point and the reference
as it is often implemented. The resistance values between different measurements are compared to determine
possible broken conductors and high-resistance connections. The use of dc removes errors due to inductive
interference from the test leads to the ground grid or system under test.

The use of dc may allow better rejection of ac noise from the station under test in the form of standing voltages
or induction due to currents or high voltages. The use of dc-based measurement can, however, include errors
where standing dc voltages are present. Such standing voltages can be from dc systems such as cathodic
protection systems, traction supplies, smelters, or simply from the small amounts of rectified current from ac
ground current.
10.6.4 Reversing polarity dc four-wire method
This method is like that presented in 10.6.3 but also includes multiple measurements of the dc voltage with the
current being reversed in polarity between each measurement. By analyzing multiple readings (with polarity
reversal) standing dc voltages are canceled and a more accurate indication of the magnitude of the standing
dc voltage can be given. The amount of variation between consecutive measurements can be used to indicate
noise. A simple version of current reversing involves doing so at a fixed frequency (synchronous current
reversing). The frequency of synchronous switching must consider the range of charging time required for
the range of systems to be tested (to avoid nonconservative errors). The resistance values between different
measurements are compared to determine possible broken conductors and high-resistance connections.

11.1 Overview
Technical report PES-TR-107 [B64] was prepared by IEEE WG6 in conjunction with the preparation of the
current edition of this guide. It provides a detailed commentary on the value and limitations of using crushed
aggregate as a resistive layer, initial and ongoing test method descriptions, as well as further details regarding
material selection and application.
11.2 Crushed aggregate use for electrical safety
For the purposes of safety in substation grounding system design, the primary role of substation surfacing
material is to limit current through the human body. The ability of the crushed aggregate layer to limit body
current is dependent on the increase in effective foot-to-ground contact resistance that the layer provides. Both
the thickness and electrical resistivity of the material impact the magnitude of the foot-to-ground resistance,
and the tolerable thresholds for touch and step voltages. Thus, the main reason for using a surface covering

different from the top layer of soil is to reduce current through the human body. In most cases, the surface layer
can be chosen to have a higher resistivity than the ground surface and introduce a contact and series impedance
to the shock circuit. With a layer of high-resistivity crushed aggregate covering the substation surface, higher
prospective touch and step voltages can present a lower risk than lower voltages with no crushed aggregate.
To date, there is no industry standard available covering how to measure the wet or dry resistivity of crushed
aggregate surface materials. This clause provides a summary of key test methods available for measuring the
electrical resistivity of crushed aggregate.
A task force of the IEEE PES Substation Committee Working Group G6 developed the technical brochure
IEEE PES-TR-107 [B64] to further the knowledge and understanding of practical test methods for testing
crushed aggregate. In addition to providing testing methodologies, the technical report provides guidance for
designers using crushed aggregate for electrical safety. Existing published methods and in-house developed
methods for resistivity measurements of aggregate were surveyed among the industry. The in-house developed
methods were developed over many years by various utilities. A test method was developed with the aid of
geotechnical laboratory experts and is included in IEEE PES-TR-107 [B64] and described in Annex F.
Given the natural variability in crushed aggregate, it may be worthwhile testing multiple samples from the
one quarry to characterize the expected resistivity range, which may be as high as 30% to 50% (IEEE PES-
TR-107 [B64]). Care must be taken regarding where or when to use the electrical resistivity of the crushed
aggregate in the grounding system design. The parameters that designers use should be based on actual values
applicable for the materials used on a site, and the environment in which the grounding system is located.
Also, adequate consideration should be given to the performance of surface layers over the full life of the
installation. Confidence in the performance of crushed aggregate surface layer to provide a safety measure will
require appropriate specification, installation, and commissioning procedures, as well as ongoing inspection
and maintenance procedures that are able to detect nonconformance.

Contaminating the surface layer will lower the resistivity of the surface material and is one of the most common
mechanisms for affecting the resistivity of the surfacing material over time. The collection of grit and organic
matter over time will lead to rotting and deposition of this waste at the lower levels of the crushed rock layer.
Over time this will build up and encompass the surrounding rock. The amount of air gap is reduced, and the
resistivity of the surface layer will be lowered.
Foot or vehicle traffic may also compress the surface rock layer into the underlying soil layer further reducing
the effective resistance of the rock layer (variable thickness).
Wind-blown salt or sand, chemical fouling, and biological fouling can contaminate the surface aggregate.
Substations located near heavily traveled highways in northern climates may be exposed to wind-blown salt
for snow and ice mitigation. Likewise, weed control herbicides used to help prevent plant growth within the
substation may provide unwanted electrolytes reducing the electrical resistive properties of the aggregate.
Unchecked plant growth within the site introduces fines and in time breaks down the aggregate layer.
Tables showing typical resistivity values for different types of surface material are for general guidance and
not for grounding design. Design values could be based on measured values of resistivity of the stone typically
purchased by the asset owner. If a design specifies the need for a layer of crushed aggregate to mitigate touch
and step voltages, it is best practice to prove by test that the aggregate to be installed at the substation has a
resistivity at least higher than the value used in the design, preferably with an added safety margin to allow for
degradation over time.
11.4 Testing methods
Recognizing the need for an industry standardized procedure for the testing of substation surface aggregate,
two test methods were developed with the sponsorship of several utilities and consultants (IEEE PES-TR-107
[B64]). Annex F contains details of sampling methods for obtaining material from a quarry as well as in an
existing site (e.g., substation). Many test methods for surface rock resistivity are described in detail in Annex F.
11.4.1 Laboratory test method
Testing methods used in the past for measuring the resistivity of substation crushed aggregate surface covering
were developed by individuals with a predominantly electrical background. When these methods are given
to a geotechnical laboratory with a sample to be tested, there can be miscommunication between parties.
There can be a disconnect between the intended testing procedure and the one used leading to considerable
variation in the reported resistivity, depending on the laboratory doing the testing. To overcome this issue, a
recommended laboratory test method was developed through a collaborative effort of the IEEE Substations
Grounding and Lightning G0 Subcommittee. The result was a repeatable test method that was first developed
with ASTM test method philosophy, which geotechnical testing firms are familiar with, and provides the
grounding designer with results that would be consistent regardless of the laboratory testing the sample. The
test method is included in IEEE PES-TR-107 [B64] and is presented in Annex F.

11.5 Application of testing methods
11.5.1 New installations
Measurement of the resistivity of a sample of aggregate supplied by the same quarry that will supply the stone
for the specific project is recommended prior to installation at a substation. After installation, a particular set
of atmospheric and soil conditions might not allow determination of resistivity under all operating conditions.
Also, unfavorable measurements made after initial placement are likely to be too late to allow adjustment of
the design of the grid and surface material. Resistivity measurement of surface material, under controlled
conditions, before placement, allows a broader range of practical modifications to be made to the ground grid
design.
11.5.2 Existing installations
Once a substation has been operating for several years, the resistivity of the aggregate layer can vary with
respect to what was assumed in its original design. If the design relied on a certain surface layer thickness
and resistivity value to meet safety targets, it is recommended that the asset owner monitors the condition of
the aggregate layer (as part of the configuration management cycle for the grounding system). The field test
method is designed to accommodate multiple test samples easier than standard laboratory test method like the
one presented in Annex F.
12. Transient impedance of a grounding system
12.1 General
Lightning protection shielding failures may occur in facilities (e.g., substations and lines). The lightning
discharge current may cause a service interruption or damage primary or secondary equipment within an
installation. Direct stroke protection is designed to limit the probability of the occurrence of a disruptive
lightning discharge by intercepting the lightning stroke and diverting the discharge current to earth through
the grounding system. Examples of standards for design and installation of lightning protection systems are
IEEE Std 998™-2012 [B58], IEC 62305:2024 [B50], and NFPA 780-2011 [B75].
The flow of the lightning discharge current through the grounding system may cause a disruptive discharge
from a grounded conductor to an energized conductor (back-flashover). The back-flashover phenomenon
generally has a higher probability of occurrence than a direct lightning stroke to an energized conductor. The
magnitude and rise time of the back-flashover voltage is a function of the lightning stroke current and the
surge impedance of the conductive path from the point of lightning impingement to remote earth. The surge
impedance is a function of the surge impedance of the ground electrode to the surrounding earth and the down
conductor path from the lightning strike point to the buried ground conductor.
When the lightning discharge current is injected into the ground, it may cause soil ionization. Soil ionization
can reduce the impedance of the grounding system. However, soil ionization is not able to fully explain the
behavior of the grounding systems under lightning conditions. In the last decade, the focus of several research
groups has been on the frequency dependence of the soil parameters and the methods for measuring the

transient impedance of grounding systems. This includes tower-footing electrodes in overhead lines as these
impact the transmission line lightning performance.
Electromagnetic compatibility and equipment transient energy withstand levels are key factors in installations
with sensitive electronic equipment (e.g., telecommunication towers including wireless services, substations,
remote equipment enclosures). Consulting adequate equipment surge withstand standards, applying good
control wiring shielding practices, and conducting equipment testing are important tools in the design of such
installations.

12.4 Substation transient interference measuring techniques
The final subclause addresses testing methods that may be used to simulate the transient energy response of
station instrumentation.
Equipment surge withstand standards, control wiring shielding practices, and equipment testing are
complementary practices when designing for electromagnetic compatibility and equipment transient energy
withstand levels. High-voltage arcs resulting from the operation of disconnect switches or circuit breakers,
lightning, and power system faults are major sources of transient surges. Other sources of radio frequency
interference (RFI) are switching of power electronic equipment (e.g., FACTs, HVDC converters). Fast and
very fast transient voltages normally have very short signal rise times, and thus, they contain a very wide
frequency spectrum. Rise times can be shorter than several nanoseconds, resulting in bandwidths that range
from power frequency (e.g., 50 Hz to 60 Hz) up to 100 MHz (CIGRE Working Group WG33/13–09 [B27],
Ford et al. [B40], Grcev [B46], Harvey [B49], Lodwig and Schuetz [B68]).
The control wiring practices utilized in substations are required to reduce high-frequency transient surges
to acceptable levels for the connected metering, relay, and SCADA equipment. Coordinated designs

more cost-effective nonspecialized electronic equipment. Another benefit is a reduction in the necessity for
exhaustive field testing of all installed wiring systems within substations.

In situ tests that evaluate the transient performance of equipment can be specified for a new installation

Instrumentation
D.1 Earth test meter
A specialized tester is used to test the unique nature of ground resistance. A dedicated ground resistance tester
can avoid the problems that can arise from the use of a dc ohmmeter or two-terminal multimeter. The tester
establishes a current through the soil from the test ground to a test probe at a distance representing “remote
earth.” An ac or white noise signal is used to reduce interference from soil transients and inductive noise, by
providing a distinct frequency against which to make the measurement. A second remote probe senses voltage
drop by soil resistance to the point of placement, and the two measured parameters of current and voltage drop
are used to calculate the resistance of the test ground. The ability to move the potential probe independently
of the current probe enables the graphing of resistance changes in the vicinity of the test ground to avoid
misinterpretations caused by localized anomalies or coupling with the current probe.
Early models operated by balancing the unknown resistance of the test ground against a series of decade
resistors of precise values. Power was supplied by an on-board, hand-cranked generator. The hand-cranked
earth tester is an instrument with an alternating current generator with a mechanical rectifier, a gear train and
crank for operating the generator, and a resistance network. The indicator is a taut band microammeter. When
current is applied to the indicator, the pointer deflects either positive (right), negative (left), or remains at zero
(center). The direction and amount of deflection is determined by the relationship of the resistance dialed into
the earth tester and the resistance under test. If the resistance under test is greater than the resistance dialed into
the earth tester, then the indicator will read positive. If the resistance under test is less than the resistance dialed
into the earth tester, then the indicator will read negative. If the resistances are equal, then the indicator will
read zero. By this process, a series of decade dials balances each known resistor in the instrument against the
load being tested until the final reading is obtained.
The alternating current generator consists of a single stator coil and a bipolar permanent magnet rotor. A
mechanical rectifier positioned on the end of the shaft converts the returning unbalanced current to dc for the
meter.
Later models employed direct-reading analog meters. When the test switch is closed, the current passing
through the electrode under test from the tester’s generator and the voltage drop between the electrode under
test and the potential probe are used as inputs to a log ratio computer. The output of the log ratio computer is
the logarithm of the unknown resistance. This signal drives the ohmmeter whose scale is calibrated directly
in ohms. A secondary function could be performed to test the resistance of the probes so that they could be
determined to be within the required measurement parameters. A second switch position enables the tester to
measure the resistance between the electrode under test and the current spike. In this mode, a separate range
network enables the meter to indicate resistance much higher than the main scale by using a separate current-
resistance subscale. The same function can be used to check potential resistance by momentarily switching
leads. The two detection circuits operate synchronously with the frequency of the test current. This enables
them to reject signals of other frequencies.
Digital models were later developed with appropriate modifications while adhering to the fundamental
schematic of a dedicated ground tester. The potential drop across the earth resistance under test is fed to the
measuring circuit via the potential terminals. An input buffer and voltage limiter help prevent the measuring
circuit from loading the earth resistance under test. The test signal is supplied by a reversing dc or constant-
current ac source powered from a floating supply that can be switched to provide appropriate ranges. Later
models auto-range by the control logic monitoring the output of the over-range detector and switching the
current source to a lower current output. These instruments also auto-range if the high current loop resistance

detector senses too much current loop resistance for the preset range. An internal relay could short one current
and one potential terminal together to provide a three-pole test for speed and convenience if desired. The test
current is passed via the current terminals through the earth under test. A crystal-controlled oscillator is at

D.2 Clamp-on ground tester
The fall-of-potential method (for grounding grid resistance test) is reliable and accurate, and it can be used to
test any size ground system. Additionally, the operator has complete control of the test setup and can check or
prove the results by testing at different probe spacings. Unfortunately, the fall-of-potential method in Clause 7
also comes with several significant disadvantages, as follows:
a) It may be time consuming and labor intensive.
b) External or peripheral ground electrodes connected to the system to be measured will affect the
resistance measurement.
The clamp-on ground testing method provides the operator with the ability to make effective measurements
under the right conditions. The clamp-on methodology is based on Ohm’s law. A known voltage is applied
to a complete circuit, and the resulting current flow is measured. The resistance of the circuit can then be
calculated. The clamp-on ground tester applies the signal and measures the current without a direct electrical
connection. The clamp includes a transmit coil that applies the voltage and a receive coil that measures the
current.
For the clamp-on method to work properly, there should be a complete circuit already in place, as the operator
has no probes and, therefore, cannot set up the desired test circuit. The operator should be certain that earth is
included in the return loop. The tester measures the complete resistance of the path (loop) the signal is taking.
All elements of the loop are measured in series. The method assumes that only the resistance of the test ground
contributes significantly.

the returns are few, or all the elements are “high,” then the error can be large.
The primary advantage of the clamp-on method is that it is quick and easy, as no probes need be driven, and
the ground rod does not have to be disconnected from the system. The clamp-on method also includes the
bonding and overall connection resistance of the system and can measure the leakage current flowing through
the system; this information is not available from a fall-of-potential (FOP) test. FOP measures only the ground
electrode, not the bonding of the system. With the clamp-on method, an “open” or high-resistance bond will
show up in the reading because the clamp-on tester uses the grounding conductor as part of the return.
There are several major advantages to the clamp-on method but also many disadvantages. It is important for
the operator to understand the limitations of the test method so that the operator does not misuse the instrument
and get erroneous or misleading readings. A clamp-on tester is an important tool in the bag of a test technician
but cannot be the only instrument used.
The clamp-on method is only effective in situations with multiple grounds in parallel, or with the tested ground
electrode connected to a very low resistance second electrode (e.g., a very large grounding system). This
method cannot be used on isolated grounds, as there is no return path, making it not applicable for installation
checks or commissioning new sites. In addition, the clamp-on method cannot be used if an alternative lower
resistance return involving the soil exists, such as with communications towers or substations.
The operator should also be aware of the subtleties of the test method to produce accurate results and analysis.
If another part of the ground system is in the “resistance area” of the electrode under test, then the result will
be lower than the true resistance of the electrode, which could lead to a false sense of security. Also, the test is
performed at a high frequency to enable the transformers to be as small as possible from a practical viewpoint.
The downside is that this approach might be less representative of a fault at power frequency than results from
the traditional ground testing frequency of 128 Hz.

D.3 Computer-based ground meter—Modified fall-of-potential method
The fall-of-potential (FOP) method (for testing ground grid resistance) is the most popular method of measuring
the resistance of ground electrodes and has been widely used for many years. However, there are many
variables and situations that can distort test results and greatly reduce the accuracy of these measurements. In
recent years, a computer-based ground meter has been developed that can moderate the effect the variables
have on test results and improve the accuracy of the measurements.
A computer-based ground meter typically consists of a computer, a variable frequency (0 Hz to 2500 Hz)
current source capable of generating 2 A to 20 A, and a multichannel data acquisition system with necessary
probes and interconnecting wiring. This method injects a series of transient electric currents between the
ground grid under test and a remote current probe with ground potential differences being measured at multiple
locations. Filters are used to remove frequencies outside the test signal frequency, test leads are calibrated, and
other measures are taken to reduce outside influences on the data being measured. The waveform sampling
of the test current and resulting potential differences are recorded by the computer including the phase angle.
Prior to performing the test, the ground grid configuration and test probe locations are drawn using an editor
provided with the computer software. Accurately depicting the configuration of the ground grid and test probe
locations to maintain good accuracy in the measurements is important.
With the ground grid modeled and test results recorded, the computer software compares the modeled voltage
gradients with the recorded test voltage gradients and performs a statistical analysis to determine the ground
resistance that best fits the ground grid model with the test data.
The major advantages of the computer-based ground meter include the following:
— The ground grid does not need to be isolated from other ground sources.
— The current electrode can be positioned a minimum of two times the grid diagonal compared with five
times the grid diagonal distance required by the traditional fall-of-potential method.
— The data being recorded are less susceptible to interference, harmonics, and induced voltages.
While the computer-based ground meter does have several advantages that are outlined in the previous list, the
user should notice that there are still areas that could moderate the accuracy of this method, such as follows:
— An accurate model of the ground grid configuration and test probe locations is essential for good results
— Above-grade or below-grade metallic paths
— Nonhomogeneous soil conditions

E.1 Aggregate sample collection
The rock may be stored after shipping in a plastic bucket with a waterproof top. The container should be
tagged properly with project number, site name, and sample number. It is good practice to keep track of the
location from which the samples were taken. Properly procured aggregate samples are crucial to determining
representative test results. The use of improper sampling techniques and equipment may affect the actual
results of the aggregate product quality. Unless instructed to do so in the testing specification, the rock should
not be washed after collection.
ASTM D75-2019 [B4] deals specifically with appropriate test methods of collecting samples of aggregates.
E.2 Aggregate sampling concerns
There are three potential sampling concerns, as follows, which may affect the way aggregate samples are
procured. The aggregate sampling concerns are related to segregation, degradation, and contamination:
a) Segregation: Segregation occurs when larger coarse particles separate from smaller, finer sized
particles. Segregation occurs anytime the combined aggregate is moved. In substations, this can
occur in areas that see snow removal operations in the winter. These operations can strip the coarser
material from the surface and pile it in different areas. Use extreme care to avoid segregated areas
while obtaining representative aggregate samples.
b) Degradation: Degradation is the physical, chemical, and environmental conditions that can cause a
breakdown of the aggregate structure. In substations, areas that regularly see vehicle traffic can have a
physical breakdown of the aggregates. Snow removal operations that use deicers can cause problems
with the chemical makeup of the aggregates as well as a physical breakdown from the repeated freeze/
thaw cycles. Sampling areas of regular traffic may contribute to a test result that is not representative
of the entire substation.
c) Contamination: Contamination occurs when foreign material is included in the aggregate sample
being procured. Contamination can consist of wind-blown debris, road salt tracking into areas from
maintenance vehicles and organics such as weeds and roots. An example of this at a substation would
be collecting the yard stone layer too deep and including the subgrade material (or whatever may be
beneath it) in with the sample. Subgrade soil would greatly affect test results for that sample.
E.3 Sample size
The test vessel size and number of samples to be tested will determine the minimum size of the sample to be
provided by the quarry. ASTM D75-2019 [B4] provides a guide for the minimum size of sample (and hence
test vessel) based on the nominal maximum size of the aggregate. If the test vessel size is adequate to meet the
ASTM requirements, then the sample size may be determined based on the number of samples to be tested
with a margin added to allow for spillage or contamination of the part of the sample.
It is good practice to test at least three samples of the aggregate to enable variations to be observed. Once
experience is gained with rock from a particular quarry the sampling requirements may be reduced or increased
as required. The size of the sample must be agreed in consultation with the laboratory or testing specialist.

E.4 Stockpile sampling
If sampling a material that is proposed for use at a substation and it is currently in a stockpile at the production
facility or on site, there are three options for sampling, as follows:
a) Observe the working face of the stockpile and obtain a sample from each quarter of the working
face of the stockpile, the working face being the face of the pile from which the aggregate is being
removed. Obtain each quarter point sample by cutting deep into the face of the pile with a loader or
other similar power equipment. Dump each quarter point sample in a separate pile, level the pile, and
take at least three shovels full to form an increment. Repeat for each quarter point and combine into
one total composite sample.
b) While not ideal, sampling a stockpile directly can be completed by obtaining samples from increments
with a square nosed shovel from quarter points of the pile perimeter at both 1/3 and 2/3 levels of slope
length from bottom to top. Increments must be obtained by holding a protective barrier above the
sample location to help prevent aggregate slide and discarding 0.25 m to 0.31 m (10 in to 12 in) of
surface aggregate (a 2 ft × 2 ft piece of 3⁄4 in plywood works well for this). A total of eight increments
must be taken.
c) If aggregate is sampled from a truck dump at the site, have a loader or other mechanized equipment,
flatten the stockpile after the truck has dumped. Obtain samples from each quarter of the flattened
stockpile. Collect additional samples in this manner from at least three other truck dumps to form a
composite sample.
E.5 In situ sampling
In situ sampling for substations should be completed in a way that will allow for representative samples to be
taken to reflect the variability across the substation. It is recommended to use the following guidelines when
sampling:
a) Inspect the entire area of the substation yard, identify areas that may have contamination
(e.g., organics, fine sediment, areas replaced with new aggregate). Also check if the drive areas have
been prepared with an entirely different material.
b) The area of greatest concern, and therefore what should be sampled, are aggregates immediately
surrounding the equipment and foundations in the substation yard.
c) Proceed to move around the perimeter of the equipment and randomly collect aggregate samples from
a range of locations in this area. When collecting the sample use a square nose shovel and select
an approximately square sampling footprint the dimensions of the shovel width. Take the sample
within this square footprint to the depth of the aggregate taking care not to contaminate the sample
with subgrade soil. When sampling there may be noticeable segregation from top to bottom. Include
aggregates that appear to be from the original yard stone placement. Knowledge of the originally
placed thickness would allow sampling to an appropriate depth.
d) Care shall be taken that the sample collected is representative of the material that has been placed in
the entire yard area. If more than one type of material appears to be present, a sample of each should be
taken, and the areas of the different materials must be noted.

that have become fouled by mineral particles such as sand, silt and clay limiting the aggregate to free drain
precipitation from the aggregate. Mixed graded aggregates tend to appear more like dense graded aggregates,
but do not fit the criteria for determination of a moisture density relationship as dense graded aggregates.
Special care for determining the unit weight and saturated moisture condition needs to be exercised when

F.1.14.2 Determining the unit density of aggregates
Open graded/mixed graded aggregates—The unit density is determined in accordance with ASTM C29-
2023 [B3]. The procedure describes methodically adding aggregate into a volumetric container and compacting
with repeated strokes of a steel rod 25 times. Aggregate is placed in three even layers until the container is
full. The top surface of the aggregate is struck off such that the protrusions of aggregate above the rim of the
container approximate the voids below the container.
Dense graded aggregates—The unit density of dense graded aggregates is determined in accordance with
ASTM D698-12-2021 [B6] or ASTM D1557-2021 [B7] that characterizes the moisture-density relationship of
the material using a uniform compactive energy. The results of the test yield a maximum density at an optimum
moisture content. The optimum moisture content represents a condition where the moisture incorporated into
the materials has aided in compacting the material to a dense condition but introducing more moisture would
displace particles resulting in lower density.
In situ density—In situ density may be measured using any number of acceptable methods. However, it
is ordinarily acceptable to determine in-place unit density using nuclear methods in accordance with
ASTM D6938-2023 [B9].
F.1.14.3 Determining saturated condition of aggregates
Determining the saturated condition of aggregates prior to performing the resistivity test will allow
preparation of test samples to a predetermined condition. Determining the saturated condition is performed
on a representative sample of the aggregate. Distilled water or tap water is acceptable for determining this
moisture condition on this preliminary sample. However, if the sample is to be incorporated into the resistivity
test sample, distilled water shall be used to determine the saturated condition.
Open graded aggregates—To determine the saturated condition of an open graded aggregate, obtain a
3500 g sample of the aggregate and inundate the sample with water for a period of 24 h in a suitable container.
After 24 h, decant the majority of the water from the sample taking care to retain fine particles. When a
sufficient amount of water has been decanted, place the sample and container on a slight incline to allow water
to drain to one end of the container. Allow the sample to drain for 15 min while repeatedly removing collected
water from the container using an aspirator or suitable suction device. Determine the moisture content of the
drained sample in accordance with ASTM D2216-2019 [B8]. Record this moisture content as the “saturated”
moisture.
The specifier may require a test conducted at the Saturated Surface Dry (SSD) condition. To determine the
SSD condition of an open graded aggregate, obtain a 3500 g sample of the aggregate and inundate the sample
with water for a period of 24 h in a suitable container. After 24 h, decant the majority of the water from the
sample, taking care to retain fine particles. When a sufficient amount of water has been decanted, place the
sample and container on a slight incline to allow water to drain to one end of the container. Allow the sample
to drain for 15 min while repeatedly removing collected water from the container using an aspirator or suitable
suction device. Place the sample on an absorbent cotton towel and remove the surface moisture by rolling the
aggregate on the towel until the surface moisture is sufficiently removed. Continue manipulating the particles
until the aggregate appears free of surface moisture and sharp edges appear to be dry, as exhibited by a lighter
color than the remainder of the particle. Determine the moisture content of the drained sample in accordance
with ASTM D2216-2019 [B8]. Record this moisture content as the “SSD” moisture.
Dense graded aggregates—The saturated condition for dense graded aggregates for the purposes of this test
is considered as the moisture condition 2% greater than the optimum moisture as determined by the moisture
density relationship per ASTM D698-12-2021 [B6] or ASTM D1557-2021 [B7]. The specifier shall identify
the method to determine the moisture density relationship of the aggregate. If not specified use ASTM D698-
12-2021 [B6].

F.1.14.5 Determining appropriate test orientation
The test orientation is a matter of preference (Figure F.3 through Figure F.9). However, tests have shown that
the horizontal method yields consistent results without the aid of copper mesh to enhance contact with the
electrode plates. The process of compacting the material into the test vessel is thought to realign the particles
on the electrode plates thereby resulting in intimate contact.
Tests conducted in the vertical orientation required the addition of copper mesh on the top electrode plate since
the action of compacting the material into the test box does not result in the realigning of the material against
the top plate. Tests using copper mesh closely represented tests conducted in the horizontal orientation. A
suitable weight is needed on the top electrode plate to maintain intimate contact with the sample.
When determining the orientation of a test vessel, if multiple layers of differing material are desired, the
vertical orientation will be more suitable. Care should be taken that intimate contact is maintained between the
material and the top and bottom electrode plates.

F.2 Field test measurement of aggregate electrical resistivity
F.2.1 Example—Field test for electrical resistivity of aggregate
F.2.1.1 Introduction
This subclause sets out a procedure for the field measurement of the electrical resistivity of aggregate (crushed
rock) commonly used as a surface treatment in and around substations. The performance of aggregate as a
series impedance layer varies with many factors including material type, particle size, impurities present,
compaction, and moisture. This testing method simulates foot compaction and tests for dry and wet estimates
of electrical resistivity.
The testing outlined in this document considers moisture content and nonquantified typical compaction. The
results of this field test provide an indication of the resistivity of the aggregate. These results are suitable for
comparative, and in many cases, isolated performance assessments supporting choices such as follows:
a) Replace the surface layer with higher resistivity material
b) Supplement the surface layer with additional suitable material
c) Clean the material and reinstate
d) The material is fine as found
Given the natural variation in material performance over seasons, and the fundamental variation in many of
the key parameters, field testing does not require and should not be claimed to have high precision. It has been
generally found that the results should be considered ±20% and applicable targets should be chosen such that
they are on the conservative side of that error bar when used for a safety analysis.

F.3 Method for measuring the apparent resistance of surface layer
F.3.1 Surface layer apparent resistance measurement
This test method may be used in situ to measure an apparent resistance of the crushed aggregate layer, and
hence allow an apparent resistivity of a layer of crushed rock to be calculated without needing to transport the
rock from the site. The actual foot-to-ground contact resistance as defined in IEEE Std 80 is not easily measured
without running long test leads. An alternative measurement that may be made with a ground resistance meter
is the resistance between a grounded structure and a disc placed on the substation surface material.
F.3.1.1 Equipment
The following steps may be followed to ascertain an apparent contact resistance and resistivity of the crushed
aggregate layer in a substation:
a) Electrode configuration: One large electrode (30 cm diameter equivalent to area of 2 ft) to provide a
contact with test current circulated to a nearby grounded structure.
b) Electrode type: Plate electrode of 300 mm diameter (or 250 mm by 300 mm rectangular plate). Use
copper wool under the plate to provide good contact with the rock (conservative position).
c) Loading resistor: No loading resistor required, unless used to measure current or as a parallel resistor
to enable higher resistances to be measured (as for dry rock conditions).
d) Moisture: Dry measurement (if taken) then wetted down using tap water.
e) Weighting: A 300 mm diameter plate is large enough to enable a person to stand on the plate and
provide the downward force required to contact the crushed aggregate. Having different people stand
on the plate enables the effect of varying body mass to be examined.
f) Current source: A 4-terminal ground resistance tester that can source a reasonable amount of current
will be adequate to measure wet conditions. If high-resistance dry conditions are to be measured
(e.g., greater than 20 kΩ ), a parallel resistor may be used or an ac current injection source.
g) Measurement locations: Several test sites should be selected across the switchyard surface, with an
aim of identifying areas of poor resistance, as well as better resistance. Also, it is of value to observe
the effect of measurements on top of the ground grid conductors and in the middle of a grid mesh
(measuring thickness of uncontaminated rock in each location). Test locations are to be shown on the
ground grid layout drawing.
h) Photographs: Photos taken showing rock condition prior to testing
F.3.1.2 Test method
The following test steps relate to considering a “wet” condition. While aggregate is not usually wet, the wet
condition is usually considered in grounding system design:
a) Markup the station ground grid layout drawing to show the test point location, paying special attention
to the location of ground grid conductors. Take a photograph showing a close-up of the aggregate
condition.
b) Liberally wet the surface of the aggregate with tap water over an area at least 500 mm by 500 mm
( 19 . 7 × 19 . 7 in).
c) Place the 300 mm diameter plate on the aggregate surface and use the assistance of a person to bed the
plate on the rock and maintain downward pressure.
d) Measure the resistance between the plate and a nearby grounded structure.
